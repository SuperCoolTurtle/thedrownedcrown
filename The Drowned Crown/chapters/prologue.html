Harl Veyr woke because the ship was humming.

At first he thought it was the wind.

The Mercy Bell had been taking a hard northern swell since midnight, and every timber aboard complained differently when the sea lifted her about. Her ribs creaked deep beneath the bunks. Barrels knocked against their lashings. Ropes strained against hooks overhead. Somewhere beyond the bulkhead a loose spoon tapped, stopped, and tapped again with each roll and jolt.

He had been aboard enough ships to know their many voices. A vessel never truly slept. Even becalmed, wood shifted against wood, water worried at the hull, and rigging whispered overhead. Sailors learned to sleep through noises that would wake landsmen screaming, but this was not one of them.

The sound came through the deck.

Harl lay still beneath his blanket, one cheek pressed against damp planking, and felt the vibration travel through his jaw before it faded. The forward hold returned to the ordinary noises of sleeping men.

Twenty-three of them lay scattered among provisions and gear, wrapped in wool or old sailcloth, boots tucked beneath their heads or wedged between barrels. The horn lantern above the ladder had burned down to a miserable yellow bead. In its swaying light Harl could make out the hunched rise of Bren’s shoulders, two bundled sailors against the bulkhead, and the pale edge of a water cask.

Someone snored. Another man muttered a woman’s name into his blanket. Harl listened and at first there was nothing. He lowered himself again. The sound returned before his head reached the deck. This time his teeth rattled softly together.

Across the hold, one of the blankets shifted.

“Who’s creeping?” Bren asked.

“No one.”

“Then stop sounding like one.”

“Did you hear that?”

There was a pause long enough that Harl knew he had.

“Hear what?”

Harl did not answer.

They waited together in the darkness.

The Mercy Bell rolled portward. The lantern swung after her. A bucket scraped a finger’s breadth along the deck and stopped against a chest. Then the note returned.

It was not loud in the manner of a bell or horn. Loudness required something striking the air, but this sonority seemed to use the ship itself.

The barrels hummed faintly as the iron hoops around them vibrated. An overturned cup trembled where someone had left it. The boards beneath Harl’s bare feet seemed to throb once, then again, and finally hold the sensation until he could no longer tell whether the sound remained or only his bones remembered it.

Bren pushed himself upright. Neither man spoke until the sound had passed.

“That,” Harl whispered.

Bren rubbed his face with both hands. He was forty-five, though the sea had been working at him since twelve and had made a much older man of him for it. Two fingers were missing from his left hand and a white scar passed beneath one ear before it disappeared into his beard. His face had always struck Harl as the sort of face that belonged carved above a grave, warding the ghosts and spirits alike.

“A whale,” Bren said.

“That near?”

“Aye.”

“Under us?”

The older man’s eyes moved toward the hull but he did not speak.

Harl knew whales. Every Morholter boy raised close enough to smell the rendering yards knew whales before he knew women. He knew the wet thunder of a blow and the slap of a frightened cow’s tail. He knew the groaning chatter of smaller whales, the high cries of calves separated from their mothers, and the sounds injured bulls made when the boats kept after them too long.

He knew the songs of the Leviathans too. Everyone did. Sometimes they could be heard from the cliffs in winter, rolling up from the dark water. Sometimes the sounds reached Blackharbor itself, and for a few breaths the rendering yards and fish markets would seem to quiet, as if a whole city had collectively remembered that its walls ended at the sea.

Those songs belonged out there, though this one seemed determined to belong inside the ship.

“Sleep,” Grumbled Bren finally as he lay back down.

“You think that’s all?”

“I ‘ink we’re poor and eleven weeks from home.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“No.” Bren turned his back to him. “It wasn’.”

Harl remained sitting until the lantern went out.

Morning did not so much arrive as loosen the dark, the eastern sky now only changed from black to iron. The Mourning Sea revealed itself slowly beneath it, long ridges of slate-colored water running toward a horizon that seemed little brighter than the night.

Harl climbed onto deck and tasted salt immediately. He pulled his wool shirt tighter at the neck and stood for a moment beside the hatch, blinking against the cold; as every morning, the wind had teeth. The deck smelled of tar, old blood, wet rope, smoke from the galley brazier and the faint permanent corruption of whale-fat that no amount of scrubbing ever removed from a hunting vessel.

The Mercy Bell rolled heavily beneath him.

She was broad-bellied, black with pitch, and good deal older than most of the men who sailed her. Twenty years of northern work had scarred her oak hull white beneath the tar in places. Her bow rose bluntly rather than elegantly. She had been built for endurance, storage and slaughter, and nobody aboard had ever mistaken her for beautiful.

One mainmast rose amidships and a shorter mast stood aft. Her square sails had been patched so often that three different shades of canvas showed whenever they filled. Six clinker-built hunting boats were nested along the rails, three to either side, their hulls narrow and shallow enough to be rowed quickly around a whale.

Casks occupied almost every open stretch of deck that did not need to remain clear for lines and movement. Some held water, ale, salt meat or dried peas. The empty ones waited for oil. Harl found their emptiness became harder to ignore each morning.

The tools of their trade lay secured nearby. Heavy iron harpoons with long ash shafts. Lances for finishing a wounded whale. Broad flensing knives, some as long as a man’s arm. Hooks. Spades. Bone saws. Coil after coil of tarred hemp line, carefully laid into tubs so that the rope would run freely when a struck whale fled. Everything aboard had a purpose. Almost everything could kill a man.

Near the stern stood the shrine. The little figure there had been carved from old whalebone, browned with oil and weather. It had no face. Its arms rested straight at its sides, and three shallow lines crossed its chest. Harl noticed someone had renewed the cord around it during the night.

The bone priests called it the Drowned Man. The men of the Tri Faith called it blasphemous and heathen filth, but fearfully touched its head for luck and safety when storms broke all the same. Harl’s mother had once asked whether he prayed to it. He had told her no and that was true, mostly.

Captain Marrik was already at the bow. He wore a sealskin cloak over a heavy brown wool coat and kept one gloved hand on the rail as he stared north. His beard had gone mostly grey, though a black streak remained stubbornly beneath the chin. He had sailed forty years and captained for seventeen. A deep burn scar puckered one side of his neck from when a pot of boiling oil had overturned in his youth.

Harl followed his gaze but there was nothing there. Nothing except the same empty water they had been hunting for three days. Before that, eleven weeks of almost nothing. When Harl was a boy, his father used to swear that a whaler could leave Blackharbor at dawn and see spouts before the evening meal. He said a man could stand atop Saint Orlan’s cliff and count whales by their breath.

Harl had heard the story often enough that he eventually decided his father had been lying. Old men often lied about abundance, he found. The ale had been stronger. The winters colder. The women prettier. The fish larger. Still, there had been more whales then. That much was true, but now the Western Grounds were thinning.

Ships were ranging farther north each year. Men who once returned before autumn were staying until frost formed on the rigging. Some crews had begun sailing toward waters Morholter charts marked only with warnings copied from older warnings. This season had been worse especially.

The Mercy Bell had taken only two ordinary whales since leaving Morholt, both small enough that Master Darron, their oilman, had cursed when he saw them hauled alongside. The first had taken most of a day to kill. The second had been a cow. Harl still remembered the calf following them for hours after its mother stopped moving, but nobody aboard discussed that.

Their shares from the two animals would barely cover provisions, advances and the percentages owed to the ship’s owners. Harl had spent most of his expected pay before they even left Blackharbor. His sister needed a roof replaced. Her husband had died the winter before, and since then Harl had seen to what he could for her and the house. His mother, less direly, had begun losing teeth and the local physicker wanted silver for some bitter tonic that did nothing but make her complain less about the pain, but he could not deny her.

So when Pell spotted a great spout far to the north three days earlier, Captain Marrik had turned the ship after it. They had been following ever since.

“Morning, corpse.”

Harl glanced aside.

Sennet grinned at him with the four empty spaces where teeth ought to have been. He was younger than Bren but somehow looked less healthy, his cheeks hollow from an old fever and his nose crooked from two separate breaks.

“You look awful,” Sennet told him.

“I didn’t sleep.”

“Bren farting again?”

“I heard it.”

Sennet’s grin thinned.

“The singing?”

Harl nodded.

“So did half the ship.”

Harl looked around. Men were working, or pretending to. Old Farro had a flensing knife across his knee and was sharpening an edge that could already have shaved hair clean. Two sailors were checking the tackle for the hunting boats a second time. Another man was retying and tying the same knot without much notice, lost in his own thought.

Nobody whistled. No Morholter whaler did. Whistling aboard was an invitation to the wrong winds. Men aboard from Valenne often laughed at the superstition until Morholter sailors threatened to break their teeth, or really did break their teeth.

“Beneath the hull?” Harl asked.

“Captain says she’s sounding.” Sennet spat overboard.

“For three days?”

“Leviathans stay down long.”

“Not three days.”

“They can,” Sennet shrugged, though uneasily. “Tell him, then.”

“Why don’t you?”

“I enjoy having all my remaining teeth.”

Before Harl could retort, the bell rang once. Every man on deck looked up. Pell was halfway up the mainmast, one arm through the rigging and the other raised toward the horizon.

“Spout!”

Captain Marrik snatched up his viewing glass at once.

“Where?”

“There! North by east!”

Harl squinted. At first he saw only mist and then, suddenly, a white column rose from the sea. It climbed higher than the blow of any common whale had a right to climb, widening as it went. For several heartbeats it hung against the grey morning like smoke from some enormous hidden fire.

A murmur moved through the crew as the blow collapsed.

“There she is,” Marrik called as he lowered the glass.

Men began cheering and smiling, Harl included, and he spotted a boyish glee on even old Bran’s face. That was the cruel arithmetic of it. A Leviathan could make a poor crew wealthy. Not quite rich like the Bone Lords, but rich enough to buy land or marry. Rich enough to pay debts and even rich enough that a man might not have to take another captain’s ship into the Mourning Sea come next spring.

The common oil alone could fill barrel after barrel. Pale oils drawn from certain organs sold to physickers for absurd sums. Bone brought almost as much as flesh. The great ribs became rafters in wealthy halls. Jawbone was carved into devotional images, handles, combs and the white tokens used in certain old marriages. And if the beast carried King’s Oil near the heart—

Harl pushed the thought away. Men had murdered brothers for less and he was sure he’d not count himself among them.

“All hands!” Marrik shouted.

The ship came awake in full at his call. Sailors scrambled into the rigging. Lines ran through blocks. Canvas cracked overhead as it was adjusted to the wind. The tiller groaned as the Mercy Bell leaned north. As she gathered speed, Harl looked one last time toward the place where the spout had been. For the briefest moment the sea there seemed darker than the water around it.

They found the Leviathan again before noon.

Pell saw it first, though nobody needed him to point after the first minute. There was a change in the birds. Gulls that had been following the Mercy Bell since morning began turning away. Not all at once. One, then another, then several. Within moments the sky ahead of the vessel was empty.

The sea seemed to flatten around them, though the wind still blew. Harl could feel it against his face and see the sails straining. Yet the water immediately ahead had become strangely smooth. Captain Marrik noticed it too, it seemed.

“Reef?” He asked his mate.

“None on the chart,” the mate said.

Bren muttered something under his breath. Harl caught only the word grave. Then, without warning and all at once, the back of it came up. At first Harl mistook it for land. A long dark-grey shape emerged beyond the bow, low and broad, its surface carrying water in great sheets. More rose behind it.

The Leviathan seemed less to surface than to displace the sea from a place the sea had occupied by mistake, as it rose still further. No one cheered this time.

The animal’s back was irregular, unlike the smooth dark curve of ordinary whales. Great ridges of dense flesh and cartilage ran along its spine, rounded and weathered, giving the creature something of the profile of a half-submerged range of hills. Pale barnacles covered entire stretches of it in colonies so old they had grown atop one another. Long streamers of weed clung in protected hollows.

Its color seemed to change with the light. At one angle it appeared charcoal-grey, at another almost blue. Along the sides, the hide faded toward the peculiar white of old bone beneath deep water. Scars covered nearly every inch of it. Some were clearly old harpoon strikes, each a small puckered crater in flesh thick enough to stop lesser irons. Others were enormous parallel gouges. Harl could not imagine what creature had made them.

One scar crossed perhaps thirty feet of its back and disappeared beneath the surface. A broken harpoon shaft protruded from the flesh near the spine. The wood of it had gone white with age, and barnacles had risen around the wound.

“How old?” Harl whispered.

Bren heard him. “Older ‘an you,” he said quietly.

“Very helpful.” Harl hissed at him.

“Older ‘an me. An’ the cap’ain.”

That silenced Harl. Captain Marrik had a decade on Bren by the least.

The Leviathan breathed as they watched. Its blowholes were farther apart than those of common whales, set atop a massive rise of skull. When the lungs emptied, twin fountains erupted upward with a sound like distant siege stones striking a wall.

The mist drifted over them, lightly sprinkling the Mercy Bell. It smelled of salt, old blood and something mineral. Like wet stone or a cave.

The creature turned and Harl watched its head begin to emerge. He had walked beneath Leviathan jaws mounted over Morholter harbor gates. He had stood inside one as a child while his father laughed and told him that the whale could have swallowed their house. Bones made scale manageable, yet Harl felt deeply betrayed by the dream they had sold him. The Leviathans were impossibly larger than even their bones suggested.

The skull was tremendously long, tapering toward a blunt prow of dense flesh. Grooves ran backward from the mouth like channels worn into cliffs. The jaw did not resemble the clean smiling curve painted by southern artists who had never seen a Leviathan alive. It was uneven, battered and heavily muscled. Dark baleen showed between the lips in immense overlapping plates, each one long enough to make a roof beam.

Its lower jaw bore a scattering of pale calcified knobs, some smooth and some broken. Harl had heard old harpooners claim Leviathans used those structures to feel the deep seabed.

Beneath the jaw hung a network of long folds, but these were thicker and fewer than those of ordinary whales, stretching nearly the length of the forward body. When the animal breathed, those folds expanded slightly. Harl had the bizarre impression of cathedral curtains being stirred by wind.

Then he saw the eye. It was small and that was somehow worse. Harl thought a creature so vast ought to have possessed an eye the size of a shield at the least. Instead it was scarcely larger than a man’s fist, black and wet, set deeply beneath an enormous ridge of flesh. Harl looked away at once. Never look into the eye of a dying Leviathan, his father had preached. This one was not dying but looking still felt wrong.

“Three preserve us,” somebody whispered.

Old Farro laughed. “The Three ain’t getting no share, boy.”

Captain Marrik turned. His expression killed the laughter.

“Boats.”

For a breath, nobody moved. Then the mate shouted it louder.

“BOATS!”

The spell broke.

Men ran. Three hunting craft were selected. There was no reason to launch all six before they knew how the beast would behave, nevermind they lacked the crew. The boats were lowered by rope tackles until their hulls struck the water.

Harl went into the second, following the three men before him. He had done it dozens of times but that did not make climbing over the rail easier. Bren followed behind him taking to the tiller and Old Farro settled in at the bow.

Farro had been harpooning whales longer than Harl had been alive. He had no surname anyone used and no home anyone knew. He spent every winter in Blackharbor taverns until his money ran out and every spring asking which ship would have him. He had stripped down to his wool shirt despite the cold. White rope scars crossed both forearms.

He selected his iron. The harpoon’s head was forged with backward barbs, the socket fitted tightly around an ash shaft almost six feet long. A short length of rope connected the weapon to the carefully coiled line in the tub behind him. Farro weighed it once in his palm.

“All right, sweet girl,” the old man cooed, looking on at the distanced beast.

“Don’,” said Bren, frowning.

Farro looked back. “What?”

“Don’ call her swee’.”

Farro smiled. “Jealous?”

“Don’ name wha’ you’re killing.”

“Not naming. Courting.”

“S’wha’ happened ‘oo your firs’ wife?”

“First three.” Old Farro spat with a high cackle.

The other men laughed, but Harl did not. Bren looked sullen.

Farro faced forward. “Pull!”

The oars entered the water. Harl worked the oars with the others, backs rising and falling together. Farro stood in front holding iron and rope, while Bren helped to guide them along the water. Behind them, the Mercy Bell remained under reduced sail, following at distance. Ahead floated an animal large enough to break all three boats without meaning to.

The first boat drew slightly ahead. Jorren stood in its bow. The Leviathan did not flee. That bothered Harl more than its size. Whales fled men. Even Leviathans fled men, his father had told him. They knew boats. They knew iron. Perhaps they did not understand death as men did, but they understood pursuit.

“Closer,” Farro murmured.

The oars dipped. Harl watched the animal’s side. Its breathing was slow. Each time it exhaled, Harl counted. One. Two. Three. Longer between each breath than seemed possible. They closed to thirty yards. Then twenty. Harl could see creatures moving across the Leviathan’s hide. Not parasites, but little silver fish. They swam close against the body, hundreds of them, holding formation beside the animal as though sheltering within some invisible current.

Fifteen yards now. Farro raised the iron. The first boat reached striking distance as Jorren bent his knees. Harl knew there should’ve been a scream but none came. Whalers screamed when the iron went in. A shout part warning, part triumph. Jorren simply threw. Harl hoped he hadn’t cursed them all.

The harpoon entered just below one of the high ridges. The Leviathan moved, reacting, as its tail came up. Harl had never seen the whole thing.

The flukes rose dripping from the water perhaps seventy yards behind the head, broader than a city gate. Their trailing edges were scarred and frayed from age. One side bore a great missing crescent, as though something enormous had once bitten flesh away and failed to finish the work. The tail continued rising and for one impossible moment it stood against the grey sky.

Then it came down. The sound was like masonry collapsing and the first hunting boat ceased to exist altogether. Timber exploded outward. Harl saw Jorren thrown perhaps fifteen feet into the air. Another of Jorrens’s boat vanished beneath the falling tail.

Water struck Harl’s boat a heartbeat later.

“Back!” somebody screamed and Harl dragged his oar.

Just as the scream came, the world went white. The Leviathan rolled, its flank rising beside them. Harl could see the texture of the hide now. It was not smooth at all. Dense wrinkles crossed the skin, some as deep as knife cuts. Barnacles grew in the creases. Here and there were circular pits where lamprey-like creatures had attached themselves, though Harl knew of no lampreys equal to such wounds.

Along the lower flank ran a line of pale oval patches. Harl stared despite himself. They were not wounds, he realized. They looked almost like closed eyes. Dozens of them, each covered by translucent skin.

One opened. Beneath was not an eye but a small recessed organ lined with pale flesh. The cavity contracted as their boat passed. Harl recoiled, his heart threatening to leap from his chest. He had been sure it was an eye. Sailors spoke of Leviathans feeling storms through the skin, and Harl figured that must’ve been how.

Several shouts of “Row!” rang out, though Farro had not moved. The old man stood staring at the Leviathan’s flank with naked wonder. Then he grinned. It was the ugliest grin Harl had ever seen. Farro threw with all the might he had. To Harl's surprise, the harpoon entered behind the fin and the line leapt alive immediately.

“Line!”

Men ducked as the hemp shrieked from the tub. Farro kicked it clear of his ankle just before the last loops vanished. The rope struck the wooden guide at the bow and began smoking.

“Water!”

A bucket went over and Harl hauled it up. They drenched the line, cooling it. The Leviathan accelerated, sending their hunting boat lurching forward so violently that two men fell backward across their benches. Then they began to fly.

The boat hammered from wave to wave behind the animal. Spray struck Harl’s face hard enough to sting. His hands locked around the gunwale.

Farro laughed into the wind. “She pulls!”

Bren stared at him, eyes wide. “Le’ go!”

Farro looked back. “What?”

“Le’ go!”

“We’ve got her.” Farro protested.

“We haven’! Le’ go!”

Farro’s smile disappeared, replaced by irritation. “You frightened, boy?”

“Yes.”

That seemed to surprise Old Farro. It surprised Harl too. Not counting Captain Merrik and Old Farro himself, Bren was the most seasoned of them. He pointed behind them. The Mercy Bell had already fallen far away.

Farro made a nasty gesture towards Bren before turning back around, but he froze with the rest of them as the sea began its song. Harl felt the first note in his feet. It travelled upward through his legs, into his spine, until the inside of his skull seemed to vibrate.

The Leviathan beneath the water was calling. Not the long mournful song heard from shore. These sounds were shorter. Three immense notes. Low, lower, then so deep Harl felt the final one more than heard it. The rope trembled with each call. Again, again, and again. Harl looked north. At first there was nothing, then, faintly, an answer came across the water.

A white spout rose against the horizon. Then another. Then another.

Sennet whispered behind him. “What in the Drowned God’s name?”

More appeared. White pillars lifting one after another along the northern edge of the world.

Farro reached for the knife at his belt, but the rope went slack before he ever found it. Everyone stared. The line floated loosely across the water.

“She’s coming up,” Bren said.

No, Harl thought, but he did not know why.

Something enormous passed beneath them. Not upward, as Bren thought, at least not truly. They were going deeper. The water beneath the boat darkened. Then the entire sea lifted. Their little craft rose perhaps ten feet without a wave as a shadow passed below.

Harl saw the Leviathan descending with almost impossible grace, its great fins extended out from the body. The fins were longer than he had understood at first. Along their leading edges ran rows of pale growths like ivory nodules. The silver fish travelled with it.

And beneath the whale—something shone. Harl leaned over. Far below, through depths that should have been completely black, he saw a faint pale radiance. Round, though not quite bright. Not yet…

Then the boat overturned and cold closed over him. Harl opened his mouth and swallowed sea. For several heartbeats there was no up, only bubbles and black water. A boot kicked past his face. He flailed. Something hit the back of his hand, wood, and he seized it. At last, he broke the surface coughing.

Bren surfaced a little ways down. “Here!” He called out.

Harl kicked toward him. Men were shouting from every direction though for what he could not make out. The first boat was debris, he knew. Only the third remained upright and was rowing hard toward them. Farro floated ten yards away, faceup. His eyes were open and Harl realized the line had twisted around one of his ankles.

“Farro!” Harl shouted.

The old man lifted his head and for one ridiculous instant he smiled. In the very next the rope drew tight and Farro vanished. There was no scream nor final curse. He had been pulled downward so violently that Harl saw his hands enter the water after the rest of him.

Between the first two boats of twelve men, only seven of them returned. The third boat had lost no one, though one oarsman had broken two fingers pulling survivors from the water.

Jorren’s arm had snapped above the elbow, Sennet’s face was opened from brow to cheek by a splinter, and young Pate shook so violently that the physicker could not get broth between his teeth. Nobody asked where Farro was, everyone knew.

Captain Marrik ordered the ship south and no one argued. The Leviathan they had struck was gone. So were two irons, all the line aboard the second boat, and the first boat itself. The crew should have mourned the equipment, but men instead stared at the northern horizon. The spouts were still there, perhaps taunting.

Harl sat against the mainmast beneath two wool blankets. His clothing had been changed, but cold remained inside him. Bren lowered himself beside him. For a while they watched sailors repair what could be repaired.

“You saw it,” Bren said eventually.

Harl kept his eyes ahead. “Saw what?”

“The light.”

Harl said nothing.

“The thing underneath.”

“No.”

Bren looked at him.

“No,” Harl repeated.

The older man studied his face, then nodded. They left it there. For nearly an hour the songs followed as they sailed south. Some were distant, others so close that the hull trembled.

The crew worked without speaking. Harl had never known sailors capable of such silence. By tradition grief made them loud. Someone would curse the dead or blame the captain and someone else would tell an obscene story about Farro and turn the corpse into laughter because laughter was easier to carry home.

But nobody did. At noon, the cook served barley boiled with salt pork. Half the men did not eat. Marrik ordered double grog and that helped some.

Harl forced down three mouthfuls and gave the rest to Pate. The boy had stopped shaking only recently. He felt bad for the kid, he was too young to be at sea. Maybe a boy of eleven, but he was especially frail. He must’ve been the younger brother of one of the other men for Captain Marrik to take him.

“You know my uncle saw one once,” Pate said.

“Saw what?” Harl found himself asking again, as he looked at the boy.

“The moon.”

Bren’s spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

Pate stared into the bowl. “He was fishing beyond White Gull. Years ago. Said there was a light below the water. My father said he was drunk.”

“Your fadder was righ’,” Bren said.

“He stopped drinking after,” Pate said earnestly. “But he drowned the next winter anyways.”

“Everyone’s uncle drowned.” Bren said, dismissive.

“Not like him.” The boy said, frowning.

“How?” Harl asked quietly. He regretted it the moment the question left his lips.

Pate lifted his eyes. “He walked into the sea.”

“Enough.” Bren said, standing so suddenly the boy flinched. He walked away with his bowl.

Harl watched him go.

Pate whispered, “He heard singing.”

Harl did not finish his meal.

By late afternoon, the sky had lowered. Clouds pressed down until sea and air seemed made from the same grey substance. The wind turned east. The mate cursed at that. Every choice of sail carried them somewhere they did not want to go.

Southwest brought them toward weather. Southeast carried them closer to the migrating Leviathans. Marrik chose southwest. They had barely completed the turn when Pell called from the mast.

“Captain!”

Marrik looked up. The boy’s voice was not loud.

“Captain!”

“Say it!” Marrik barked.

“There are whales ahead!”

Marrik lifted his glass, but Harl did not need one. A back rose directly in their path. Then another, followed by three more.

Marrik ordered west. Spouts appeared there too.

East. More.

The Mercy Bell slowed. The Leviathans were not surrounding them in any deliberate circle, even Harl could see that. That might’ve been easier, even, he thought. They were all simply moving Northward.

The ship had turned directly across their path. One surfaced perhaps fifty yards away. Another farther behind. Then two more beyond those.

There were more animals than Harl had ever imagined existed. Some were enormous and some smaller. Several calves travelled beside larger adults, though even the calves dwarfed ordinary whales he observed.

Their bodies were not identical between them either, he noticed. One was nearly black and deeply scarred, another had hide so pale and perfect that it appeared ghostly beneath the water. One old animal carried an entire white forest of barnacles along its head. A younger one behind it had lost half a fluke. They breathed in unison, again and again, and the sea became mist.

The Mercy Bell drifted among them like a babe’s toy released into a procession of giants. Nobody spoke. One of the younger sailors began to pray, just barely choking the words. Harl could not tell whether the prayer belonged to the Three or the Drowned Faith. Perhaps the sailor could not either.

A Leviathan passed close along the starboard side and Harl approached the rail despite himself. The animal’s head surfaced. Harl noticed its skin was darker than the others, but it had such depth too. Along its jaw grew those same pale sensory nodules, hundreds of them disappearing beneath the water. Old scars intersected across its mouth. Its baleen was almost black.

The eye appeared suddenly as Harl's father came to mind. Don’t look, he had used to say. But he had looked. The eye was black and Harl thought there was nothing miraculous about it. Nothing supernatural. It reflected the grey sky, the ship, and Harl himself leaning over the rail no differently than his own eye would’ve done. Yet the animal held there. Its eye remained above water for three breaths. Harl could not escape the sensation that something enormous had paused to inspect something very small.

Then the Leviathan sank back into the water.

“Harl...”

Harl turned and found Bren staring at him. He looked scared.

“You’re bleeding.” He said.

That surprised Harl, and he touched beneath his nose. True enough, his fingers came away red. Sennet cursed nearby as blood ran from one of his ears. Another sailor wiped his nose. Pell cried out from the mast. Everyone looked up, though he was pointing downward.

Harl moved back to the rail. At first he saw only dark water, then slowly his eyes adjusted. The sea had become strangely transparent, not clear in any natural sense as the surface remained grey, yet he could see depth. Far too much depth.

Leviathan bodies passed below them at different levels, some dark silhouettes, others pale and distinct. Their great fins moved slowly. Schools of silver fish followed. Below them was darkness. And within that darkness—the light. The pale circle waited far beneath, perfectly round. So distant that it should not have been visible.

Yet Harl saw details upon it. Dark pits and plains and scars, craters and bumps. A moon. Under the sea.

“No,” He said, the words barely leaving his lips.

All the Leviathans stopped singing. Every voice vanished at once and the silence that followed was so sudden and jarring it was somehow worse. Captain Marrik whispered something as the ship trembled. A cask rolled half a foot and slammed against its ropes.

“Below us,” Bren said.

The second impact lifted the bow, throwing men along. A barrel broke free and rolled across the deck, crushing one sailor’s ankle before striking the rail.

“She’s holed!” Someone cried out.

Water burst through the forward hatch as men ran. Harl followed Bren below. The smell of fresh seawater reached them first. A breach had opened between two planks. Black water came through in a sheet wide as a man’s chest.

Sailors pushed sailcloth against it dutifully as others drove wooden wedges into seams. A carpenter shouted for braces.

“Pump!”

The wooden bilge pumps began working as men hauled the long handles up and down. Water poured from the outlets above. Harl joined the bucket chain with Bren. Cold water reached his ankles. Then it reached his shins. It was rising too quickly.

“There’s another breach!” The carpenter’s voice came from farther forward.

Someone shouted that he could not reach it. The ship shuddered again and the lantern fell, allowing darkness swallowed the hold. Many of the men screamed. For several moments, the only light came from the open hatch above. Then someone brought another lantern.

In the light, the forward ribs of the vessel were bending. Harl had never seen wood move like that. Oak beams thick as his thigh flexed inward and slowly returned as something scraped along the outside of the hull. It wasn’t a strike, if it had been they’d be surely dead. The sound lasted five seconds, though to Harl it seemed an hour.

Bren crossed himself in the southern manner. Harl stared at him, incredulous.

“I thought you hated the Three.”

“I hate drowning more.”

“Aye, then it’s the Drowned God you want,” came another voice from the dark.

Before anyone could retort, a roar came from above. Everyone looked upward as they scrambled onto deck. The mainmast had gone down across the starboard side. Rigging dragged into the water. Canvas filled and pulled against the broken spar like a sea anchor.

“Cut it loose!” Called Captain Marrik’s voice from somewhere.

Many knives appeared as men hacked at the rope while others patiently worked it. It had begun to rain sometime as they worked, though Harl could not remember exactly when. It came sideways now, needling his face.

Harl could now see Captain Marrik standing near the stern. He was staring toward the shrine. Then suddenly he ripped the faceless whalebone figure from its cord and held it in one hand.

“Bring the heart!” He barked.

Nobody understood. Calls of “What?” could be heard around the crew.

“The heart!” He shouted again.

Bren’s face changed. “Wha’ hear’?”

Marrik looked toward the hold. “The cow’s heart. Bring it.”

For one moment nothing happened. Then Harl understood. He meant the second whale. The cow. Although only a regular whale, by old Drowned custom the heart should have been cut free, washed and returned to the sea before sunset. The ritual was older than Blackharbor. Some captains followed it. Others did not.

Bren stepped toward Marrik. “You kep’ it?”

“We needed the money.”

“You kep’ the fucking hear’?” Bren said again, louder this time.

“I said bring it!”

Two men disappeared below. They returned dragging a small barrel. Marrik seized an axe and split the lid. The smell spread instantly. Brine. Salt. Old blood. Preserved flesh. Inside lay the whale’s heart, collapsed somewhat under its own weight and darkened by curing salt.

The crew stared. Somewhere in the back, Harl thought he heard Pate begin crying. Marrik plunged both hands into the barrel. He lifted the heart. It took both arms. Blood-brine ran down his coat. He staggered toward the broken rail.

“I return what was taken!” He shouted into the wind as rain struck his face. “I return what was taken!”

He threw the heart and it hit the sea heavily with a splash. For several moments it floated before rolling, after which it then slowly sank. Everyone waited with bated breath, but nothing happened. Marrik stared at the water and Harl realized something just then. The captain had not expected nothing. Marrik had truly believed.

The old man began laughing. A strange, exhausted laugh. A wave struck broadside and Marrik disappeared over the rail. Harl saw his hand once. Then the sea closed. No one went after him. No one could, anyway.

The Mercy Bell took a long time to die. That surprised Harl. He had always imagined a sinking ship simply disappeared all at once. Instead she fought. The pumps worked until two men collapsed. Buckets moved until there was nowhere useful to throw the water because each wave brought more across the deck. Carpenters hammered planks over breaches they could no longer reach from the outside. Sailors stuffed wool, sailcloth and even clothing into seams.

The ship sagged lower by degrees as water invaded the hold. The deck developed a permanent list. Loose objects were thrown overboard. Barrels, spare timber, stone ballasts. Anything they could move. Men cut the remaining hunting boats free. One lowered successfully and nine men immediately crowded into it.

The mate yelled for three to get out but nobody moved. The boat struck the ship’s side hard enough to crack a plank just before one of the davit ropes snapped. The craft tipped and men fell into the sea, before the boat dropped from the broken ropes over them.

Harl watched the boat overturn below. They had four boats left in truth, though nobody tried brave another. One sailor tied himself to an empty oil cask. Another knelt near the broken mast and confessed to murdering his brother twenty-one years before. Pate crouched against the rail calling for his mother.

Harl found Bren near the bow. The older man was wrapping rope around his waist.

“What are you doing?”

“Gettin’ sum to hold.”

“That’ll drag you down.”

“It all drags you down,” Bren said wisely.

“What do we do?” Harl asked.

Bren looked past him toward the sea. “Drown,” he said with a shrug.

Harl laughed, though he did not mean to. Even Bren laughed too. But the laughter only lasted a moment. Ahead, a Leviathan surfaced violently. Harl noticed, horrified, that Farro’s harpoon remained buried near the fin. This was the Leviathan they had struck. The animal rose slowly and Harl could see another old white shaft still embedded near the spine as well. It had perhaps carried that first weapon for twenty years. Perhaps fifty. Nobody aboard could know.

Water poured from its head. As the Leviathan lifted into the swell, its enormous throat expanded. The folds beneath its jaw opened wider. Between them Harl could see channels of pale tissue that caught what little daylight remained. Along those folds rested lines of small translucent structures, each pulsing faintly with blood. They were the same sensory pits Harl had seen along the flank opened and closed before. The animal seemed covered in organs designed to perceive parts of the world long unavailable to men.

Its jaw opened and black baleen parted. Beyond it was darkness. The sound that eventually came out was not loud, though Harl felt it through the ruined ship. His vision blurred. Men around him clutched their ears. Some screamed and some fell.

The sound deepened and Harl knew his nose was bleeding again. As it continued, the Leviathan’s eye surfaced only just. Slowly, it opened its eyes.

Bren whispered beside him. “Don’t.”

It was too late though. Harl looked, just like he looked before, and suddenly all around him disappeared. The rain vanished and the screaming ceased and Harl could spot no broken vessel. He stood somewhere else.

Black water stretched beneath a sky he had never seen. Stars hung where no stars should be. Not quite above, but everywhere. A forest grew in the distance without soil. Pale trunks rose from black water, leafless and impossibly tall. Beyond them hung an upside down castle. Its towers descended toward the sea.

There was a beach too. Harl did not know how he reached it, only that he was there. Black sand lay beneath his feet. A door stood upright near the water. There was no wall, house, or path in sight. Just the door. With a start, Harl realized someone waited beside it. A man perhaps, or something occupying approximately the shape of one.

Harl could not see its face but he knew with the certainty of dreams that if he walked closer, it would speak. The figure lifted one hand and Harl thought he was beckoning to him. No, Harl realized as he turned, behind me.

Leviathans moved through the black water. Hundreds and thousands, descending all at once. Their enormous bodies sank toward a pale moon beneath the surface. The moon was impossibly large. Much larger than the one Harl had seen beneath the Mourning Sea.

As he watched, he understood that the dark shapes upon its face were not craters. They were openings of sorts, he thought. Something moved inside one. The figure behind him spoke, but Harl did not know if it was him to whom he was speaking. Harl never remembered the words.

Then he was back. Harl screamed like he had not done in many years. The Mercy Bell had rolled nearly onto her side. Men were sliding across the deck helplessly. Harl caught a line and Bren seized his forearm.

“Hold!” He shouted.

As Bren pulled Harl up, the Leviathan had vanished once more beneath the water. For a moment there was only rain, but then the ship was rising and creaking. Not with a wave, the Mercy Bell had been lifted from below. The bow climbed upward as loose barrels rolled aft. Men screamed and clawed at ropes. Bren and Harl hung from the line as the deck became a slope beneath them.

Then the Leviathan turned and suddenly the Mercy Bell fell from its back. Her keel struck the water sideways, splitting the hull open forcefully. Harl heard the sound through everything. A deep tearing crack as oak joints failed one after another. The sea came in, not through leaks now, but through the open body of the ship.

The deck separated beneath the mainmast. Bren’s grip vanished and Harl fell. Water closed over him. He could not tell which way was up amidst the confusion and wild water. Something struck his shoulder and he spun. The remains of the Mercy Bell drifted downward around him. A broken oar. A length of rope. A barrel. A man’s boot. A body.

Harl kicked, spurred by the sight. His lungs and legs burned but he did not relent. He found the surface, or so he thought. Harl’s hand struck planking and he pushed sideways, up, down. It made no difference that he could tell.

As the ship groaned around him air escaped broken compartments in enormous silver clouds. Harl followed the bubbles desperately, they’d lead him up he knew. He kicked and kicked until his head broke the surface. Rain struck his face once more.

As he looked at the sea around him, it had become no more than wreckage. The sea had become wreckage. Men shouted, some clinging to timber. Others swam toward anything that floated. The stern of the Mercy Bell remained above water perhaps thirty yards away. The bow had already disappeared.

“Harl!”

Harl turned towards Bren's voice. The older man clung to a section of deck and he swam toward him. A wave rolled between them, however, and when it has passed Bren was gone.

“Bren! Damn it, Bren!” Harl shouted, alone and afraid. Bren’s voice never came again.

“HARL!”

Another voice, Sennet this time. He was hanging across an oil cask, blood running from his wounded face. Harl reached him and together they held the barrel. Pate surfaced nearby.

“H-help—” The young boy cried.

Harl stretched out one hand and Pate swam to it. Before he could reach Harl though, something moved beneath him. Not the Leviathan but a long and pale shape. Thin. One of the strange silver fish. Alongside it dozens more appeared as they began to circle Pate.

“They’re touching me!” The boy screamed.

“They’re fish!”

“Get them off!” He begged.

Harl grabbed his wrist. For a moment he had him and then the sea beneath them brightened. All three men looked down. It was close now. Too close, Harl thought. Besides him Sennet had stopped breathing.

“No,” the younger man had managed. The word came out small.

The water around them became clear again and they could see the Leviathans were descending. Dozens passed through the depths. Even the struck Leviathan went with them, Old Farro’s harpoon still in its side. Harl watched until the whale’s enormous body crossed the pale circle. For a brief moment its silhouette lay black against the moon before it continued downward.

It did not go toward the moon though, it went through it. The animal disappeared and Harl forgot to hold the barrel. A wave tore it from him as Sennet shouted. Pate’s hand slipped from Harl’s and the sea separated them once more.

Harl went under again but this time he did not fight immediately. The pale light surrounded him. Above, what remained of the Mercy Bell burned as a dark shape against stormlight. Below, Leviathans passed silently toward someplace that was not the bottom of the sea.

Harl kicked upward, his chest screaming. A shape passed beside him and a horrified Harl knew it was the one they had struck. It should have gone, yet there it was. Its great head emerged from darkness as it fixed Harl with its eyes. He returned the gaze.

There was no black castle, or beach, or door. Only himself reflected in the eye. Small and drowning. For one absurd moment, Harl thought the creature looked tired.

It was old beyond anything he understood. A body that had carried scars longer than his family had carried its surname. A creature that had crossed the Mourning Sea before Blackharbor had walls. A thing men had followed with boats, iron and greed because they had looked upon something ancient and decided the proper question was how much oil might be rendered from it.

Then the Leviathan began to sing. The note invaded Harl’s bones as others below answered with song of their own. The entire sea became one sound. Harl opened his mouth and the last of his breath escaped in silver bubbles.

He thought of his sister’s roof. His mothers medicine. The narrow lane behind their house in Blackharbor. The rendering smoke that never left the washing. Bren complaining that every captain was a thief. Farro laughing as the rope ran.

The light beneath grew brighter and Harl sank. The songs followed him. Above, somewhere beyond sight, the last timbers of the Mercy Bell slipped beneath the Mourning Sea. Nothing remained to mark where twenty-four men had been. No mast, no boat and no bell. Only a few empty casks rolling between the waves.

By dawn, the storm would scatter even those. Below them, where no living sailor could follow, the second moon shone through the dark. Around it the Leviathans passed northward and downward, one after another, as though obeying a road older than the sea.

Harl watched them until his eyes could no longer see. The final thing he felt was the song. The final thing he understood was that they had never been fleeing men.

They were going home.
