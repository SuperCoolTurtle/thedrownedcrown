Consciousness returned to him by degrees, and every one of them bit. The pain came first. It had settled behind his eyes and driven roots deep into his skull, a great dull pounding that seemed determined to split his head from brow to nape. After that came the sickness, hot in his belly and sour at the back of his throat. Then the motion. Up and down, up and down, a slow and ceaseless rising beneath him that made the darkness itself seem to sway.

When at last he opened his eyes, he almost wished he had not. A flame danced inside a little glass lamp beside the bed, guttering whenever the room shifted. Its light was a poor, yellow thing, barely strong enough to push back the dark. By it he could make out rough timber walls, stained black in places and grey in others, the boards warped by years of damp and collected salt. Somewhere beyond them wood creaked against wood.

He was lying on a bed. That realization came strangely late as his bearings returned. His fingers stretched out, feeling blindly beside him. They found only coarse canvas stretched over a lumpy mattress. He shut his eyes again. There was something thick inside his head, thicker than the pain, a fog through which every thought had to crawl.

He tried to remember where he was. Nothing came. He tried to remember how he had come there. Nothing. He tried to remember—

The darkness took him again.

How long he slept, he could not have said. When next he stirred, the lamp still burned and the floor still rolled beneath him. There was a man in the corner, he realized, seeing the eyes first. They caught the lamplight when he turned his head, two small gleams floating in the dark. The man sat sprawled in a chair much finer than anything else in the little chamber, one leg crossed lazily over the other. He had apparently been watching as he slept.

Their eyes met and the stranger smiled. Fear came quickly then. The wounded man planted one hand against the mattress and tried to push himself upright. Pain tore through his shoulder as he gasped and fell back.

“Wouldn’t recommend that just yet.” The stranger was beside him before he quite understood that the man had risen.

He pressed the back of one hand against his brow. He was a thin fellow, almost wispy, with the sort of body that looked as though a strong wind might carry it off. Dark hair fell loosely about a narrow, sharp-featured face, longer around the ears and untidily swept back from his brow. There was a certain handsomeness to him, though not the clean, courtly sort; his features were too sharp for that, his cheeks a little hollow, his nose prominent, and there was something perpetually amused about the corners of his mouth. A short growth of dark stubble traced his jaw.

He was dressed considerably better than the room suggested he ought to be. A long coat of charcoal wool hung nearly to his knees, narrow through the waist and divided below it for ease of movement, its front drawn together somewhat crookedly by a row of small tarnished clasps. Salt and weather had faded the cloth along the shoulders and hem, though it had plainly been expensive once and was still kept with some care. Beneath it showed a fitted jerkin of dark, wine-colored leather over a high-necked linen shirt, the latter an almost startling cream against everything else he wore. His trousers were close-cut and dark, disappearing into worn calf-high boots. A narrow belt crossed his waist, crowded with a purse, a small sheathed knife, and several little leather cases.

Two silver rings caught what little light reached the cabin as his hand rested against the wounded man’s forehead. His touch was cool, though the rings were colder where they met flesh.

“Mm.” Apparently satisfied, he withdrew his hand. “No fever. That’s something.” He crossed to a narrow shelf fixed against the wall, the skirts of his coat shifting about his legs as the ship moved beneath them, and began pouring amber liquor from a squat bottle into two small glasses. “Took quite a beating, you did. Arrow through that shoulder of yours.” He poured the second glass. “And a damned hefty knock to the head besides.”

The wounded man stared at him, then turned toward his shoulder as though expecting to find one still sticking from it. There was none, only bandaging and the deep, throbbing ache beneath it. In his nausea and half-wake, he had not noticed it before.

The stranger was telling the truth. His body knew that much, though his mind knew nothing. He recalled no arrow. No battle. No face of whoever had struck him. No road leading here. No yesterday. Nothing.

The man returned and held out one of the glasses. “Do you know your name, son?” The stranger asked, lightly.

He looked at the drink, then at him. His name. There ought to have been an answer. He searched for it and found, again, nothing.

“I don’t know,” he admitted. The words sounded small as he took the glass. “I can’t…” His brow tightened. “I can’t remember anything.” Saying the words aloud made him somehow feel even hollower.

“Ah.” The man’s expression changed. Not greatly, but enough. He returned to his chair with the look of someone who had hoped not to hear an answer he had already expected. “Yes. I feared as much.”

The wounded man forced himself upright more carefully this time, keeping his injured shoulder still. Even that small effort set his head pounding.

“As I said, you took a hard blow.” The man rolled his drink thoughtfully between his fingers. “Truth be told, it’s something of a wonder you’re alive at all. An inch or two lower and that arrow would’ve found your heart.”

He nodded because he could think of nothing else to do. He searched his mind again, but failed to call forth anything meaningful. Not his mother’s face. Not his father’s. Nor a childhood bed or a favorite meal or the sound of a friend’s laughter. There was not even darkness where those things ought to have been just… absence.

“Where are we?” He asked.

“The Pottermore,” supplied the man.

He stared at him and the man’s eyebrows rose.

“A boat,” he clarified. Then, after a moment, “You do know what a boat is, yes?”

He frowned. That was the strange thing. He did know what a boat was. A vessel that traveled upon water. Timber hull. Mast. Sail. Deck. Rudder. He knew there were oceans and rivers and harbors. He knew men drowned if they breathed water. He knew fire burned and winter was cold and arrows were ordinarily not meant to be lodged in one’s shoulder. He knew a thousand things. He simply did not know himself.

“At least vaguely.”

“Promising.” The man said, before taking a drink.

“Why? Why do I know that?”

“Know what?” Asked the man, tilting his head.

“What a boat is.” He struggled to put the wrongness of it into words. “I know what it is, but I don’t remember one… not really. I don’t remember learning the word. I don’t remember seeing one. I know what a boat is, but I don’t know my own name.”

The stranger leaned farther back in his chair. For once, the easy amusement left his face.

“My best guess?” he said. “You’ve lost the things that made you, well, you. Names. Faces. Places. Memories. Personal things.” He tapped one finger against his glass. “The rest remains. Speech. Knowledge. How to eat without choking yourself. What a door is. What a boat is, so on.”

“That can happen?”

“Clearly.”

He did not smile and the man sighed.

“The mind is queer country, my friend. I’ve seen men forget half their years and remember every word of song their mothers sang upon their deathbed. I’ve seen another lose his wife’s name but remember every debt he’d ever been owed.” He drank. “You may remember tomorrow. You may remember a month from now. Little pieces may return one by one.”

“And if they don’t?”

The man studied his cup.

“They may not.” The man said as he studied his cup.

The words were softly spoken, and that made them worse.

“The mind is a strange thing,” he said after a while. “A very strange thing.”

A chill crept through him despite the warmth of the cabin. He supposed he ought to mourn. That was the peculiar horror of it. A man had been taken from him, an entire life murdered without leaving a corpse, and he could not even say whether he had liked the fellow. Even worse, he was the fellow.

Maybe there had been a mother who loved him. Maybe she was dead. Perhaps he had a wife. Perhaps he hated her. For all he knew, somewhere there was a man who would gladly slit his throat for all the wrongs he’d done unto him. How did a man grieve for people who meant no more to him than names he had never heard?

His head began pounding harder. Enough, he thought and he looked instead at the amber liquor in his hand and drank. The first swallow burned. He coughed violently, which made his shoulder hurt, which made him curse, which made the thin man laugh. His eyes watered. His nose ran. Whatever vile spirit had been poured for him clawed its way down his throat and settled like a little fire in his belly.

He drank the rest anyway. Warmth followed. Then, blessedly, a measure of clarity. The thin man watched all this with poorly concealed amusement.

“Well. Now that you’ve got the general run of things, I suppose I ought to tell you who I am.” He stood, bent himself into an extravagant little bow, and spread one hand. “Gustavo Kirstein, resident physicker of the Pottermore, at your service.”

“Thanks.”

Gustavo straightened, but something occurred to the wounded man before he could move from his spot.

“Wait,” he called and the man stopped. “You wouldn’t happen to know my name?”

Gustavo smiled. For the first time since he had awakened, the man looked genuinely pleased to have something good to give him.

“Ah, as it happens, I do.”

He waited.

“The folk who handed you over to us—paid us, if I’m being entirely honest—were rather sparing with their explanations. They did give us a name, though.” Gustavo raised his glass toward him. “Polonius Fritz.”

Polonius Fritz. He turned the name over silently. Nothing stirred. No flash of recognition. No buried voice whispering yes. It might have belonged to the man in the next cabin for as well he knew it.

“Polonius Fritz,” he repeated.

“That’s you.”

“Was me, maybe.”

Gustavo’s smile thinned.

“Fair enough. Who you were before, I cannot say. Given the circumstances, you could call yourself anything you pleased. No one aboard this ship would know enough to argue.”

Polonius considered that. A new name.There was something tempting about it. If the old man was lost, why wear his name? Yet he found himself shaking his head.

“No,” Polonius said.

“No?” Mused Gustavo.

“I’ll keep it.” He said the name once more inside his head. Polonius Fritz. “I rather like it.”

“A good name,” Gustavo agreed. He started for the door, then stopped with one hand nearly upon it. “Oh.”

Polonius looked up.

“That reminds me. You had something when they brought you aboard.” Gustavo reached inside his coat and produced a thin envelope. “A letter. It was unopened and addressed to you,” Gustavo explained as he handed it over.

Polonius took it. There was blood upon the paper, dried now into brown-red speckles. His blood, presumably. More curious was the seal. Someone had pressed their lips against it before closing it, leaving the faint impression of a kiss.

He ran his thumb across it. “Well,” he said, “can’t see what good it’ll do me now.”

“Perhaps more than you think.”

“Perhaps.”

Gustavo nodded. “Captain reckons another week to Blackwater, give or take.”

Blackwater. Again, it meant nothing to Polonius. Gustavo must have seen it on his face.

“No?”

Polonius shook his head.

“Thought not. I won’t spoil it for you, then.” The thin man’s eyes went distant for a moment. “What I wouldn’t give to see that city again for the first time.” There was something almost boyish in his expression then, some private memory Polonius could only envy. “If you need anything, find me. Otherwise, take it easy. You’ve already nearly died once. No sense making a habit of it.”

Then Gustavo Kirstein was gone. The door closed and silence returned in his absence.

Well, not true silence. The Pottermore had a thousand little voices of its own. Timbers groaned. Water hissed along the hull. Somewhere overhead came the muted thump of footsteps. Rope strained and wood complained.

Polonius looked down at the envelope. A kiss. Whose? A stupid question. He was no more likely to remember the owner of those lips than he was to sprout wings and fly the rest of the way to Blackwater.

He shifted carefully beneath the blankets and opened the envelope. A wilted white rose fell into his lap. Polonius stared at it. He lifted it carefully by the stem and brought the dead flower to his nose. Some little trace of its perfume remained.

Something moved inside him. Not a memory, less than that. A feeling that he had smelled this scent before. He waited, though as ever nothing came.

“Damn you,” Polonius muttered, though whether he meant the rose or his own mind he could not have said. He laid it beside the empty glass. Then he unfolded the letter.

For some reason, guilt came over him. It felt like reading another man’s correspondence. It is addressed to you, he reminded himself. Except that he wasn’t quite certain he was that man anymore. He began to read.

Dear Polonius,

I hope this letter finds you well. I know it has been some time since we spoke, but I wanted to reach out and let you know that I am thinking of you. I never stopped, really.

This morning I learned from Polynices that you did indeed leave Blackwater to pursue your dreams. I never told you, but I always feared those dreams would carry you away from me.

I would have liked to see you once more. I would have liked to look into your eyes and tell you the things I do not know how to put into a letter, but the time for that has long since passed.

Your brother was reluctant even to tell me that you had gone. He insisted you wanted nothing more to do with me.

Is that true, Polonius?

I hope it isn’t.

Whatever they said of me, whatever they told you I had done, it isn’t true. How can you not know that?

I don’t know if you even want to hear from me, but I had to write. I miss you so much. I miss your smile, your laugh, your warmth. I miss everything about you.

I needed you to know that I do not hate you. I do not begrudge you, regardless of what you may feel for me.

If there is anything I can do to help you, anything at all, please tell me. I will be here for you.

Take care of yourself, Polonius.

I love you.

Always,

Vivienne

He dropped the letter. For a moment Polonius regarded it as he might have regarded a snake discovered beneath his blankets.

The room seemed to tilt. This time he doubted the sea was to blame.

Vivienne. There was a woman named Vivienne, and she loved him. Or had loved him. No. The letter was plain enough.

I love you.

Polonius picked it up again. He read the troublesome passage twice.

Whatever they said of me, whatever they told you I had done, it isn’t true. How can you not know that?

There had been some quarrel, then. More than a quarrel, perhaps. Someone had told him something about her. He had believed it. If Vivienne was to be believed, he had left Blackwater without so much as seeing her.

Had he loved her? He searched himself. Nothing. He looked at her name again. Vivienne. It might as well have been scratched onto a gravestone belonging to a stranger.

That frightened him more than the arrow had. Then there was Polynices. His brother. Polonius had a brother.

Somewhere in the world there walked a man who shared his blood, who knew his childhood, who could perhaps tell him what food he hated and what made him laugh and whether he snored and who had first broken his heart. Polonius knew none of those things.

For a brief moment he wondered if the letter might be false. Some jest, perhaps, or part of some story whose meaning he did not understand, but that thought did not survive long. The letter had been addressed to him. He had been carrying it when he was wounded. There was blood upon it. What reason had he to doubt the only piece of his old life that remained?

Anger rose suddenly inside him. He wanted something to strike, though his shoulder would never permit it. Instead he groaned and pressed his fingers against his eyes until little lights danced behind them. Perhaps he had been a terrible man. That possibility came easily enough. The letter did him few favors.

He had apparently abandoned a woman who loved him without even granting her a farewell. Worse, he seemed to have done so because someone had whispered something poisonous into his ear and he had chosen to believe them rather than her.

Perhaps Polonius Fritz had been a fool. Perhaps a coward. Perhaps worse. It was an unpleasant thing to inherit a dead man’s sins when the dead man happened to be yourself.

Carefully, he folded the letter and the rose went back inside with it. He sealed neither. There seemed little point. Of everything Vivienne had written, only one thing connected itself to his present life: Blackwater. Gustavo had said they would reach it within a week. Vivienne wrote as though Polonius had lived there. His brother was there, or had been. Vivienne might still be there as well.

Yet even the city’s name awakened nothing in him. No street. No smell. No tower rising against the sky. Nothing. Perhaps he wanted to see it only because Gustavo had spoken of it with that faraway look in his eyes.

What I wouldn’t give to see that city again for the first time.

Well, Polonius supposed he would soon have that privilege. He lay back against the mattress. The ship rose beneath him and fell again.

Vivienne. No, the girl, he corrected himself. Calling her by her name seemed too intimate. The girl. Polynices. His brother. Polonius Fritz. Himself. They were names belonging to a story someone else had told him.

His eyelids grew heavy. Perhaps the man he once was deserved to be forgotten. Perhaps there were good reasons he felt nothing for these people. Perhaps losing himself had been some strange mercy.

The lamp flame danced beside him as the Pottermore creaked onward through the dark. And with Vivienne’s letter lying beside his bed, Polonius Fritz surrendered once more to sleep.

Sleep brought him nothing. Polonius had half hoped for dreams. Instead there was darkness, deep and uninterrupted, and when he opened his eyes again the lamp had burned itself nearly dry.

Morning was announced to him by noise, not light. Boots hammered overhead. Men shouted to one another in words he could hear but not make out, followed by the heavy scrape of something being dragged across the deck. The Pottermore rolled beneath him with considerably less gentleness than before. His stomach rolled with it.

He managed not to be sick until he stood. The accomplishment lasted perhaps only three seconds. By the time Gustavo found him, Polonius was sitting on the floor beside the bed with his back against the wall, wearing no more than a loose linen shirt and breeches, regarding a bucket between his knees.

“You look well,” said Gustavo as he shouldered the door aside with a bundle tucked beneath one arm. He tossed it onto the end of the mattress without ceremony. A loose boot tumbled to the floor. “There. What remains of you.”

Polonius looked down. For a moment he did not understand.

“My clothes?”

“What the arrow left of them,” Gustavo nodded.

Polonius reached for the coat first. It had once been a handsome thing. It was well made and unmistakably chosen rather than merely owned. Heavy charcoal-grey wool formed the body, long enough to fall almost to his knees, with a close waist before the skirts divided behind for riding or walking. Dark leather reinforced the shoulders and ran narrowly along the side seams where weather and wear would be worst. The front closed slightly off-center beneath a row of small blackened-metal clasps, several of which had been torn loose.

There was no embroidery. No fur. No bright thread announcing a household or lord. The wool was dense and tightly woven. The leather was supple. Even after blood, seawater and rough handling, the stitching remained stubbornly intact. The left shoulder had been ruined, however. Someone had cut through cloth and lining to reach the wound beneath, leaving the upper portion hanging open in a stiff, darkened flap. Blood had soaked through almost to the breast and dried there in several shades of brown.

Polonius ran his thumb along the edge. He had hoped touching it might produce something—a street, a shop, himself standing before a looking glass while someone adjusted the shoulders. But, expectedly, nothing came and there was only wool beneath his fingers.

Underneath lay a dark doublet of finer wool, cut close through the torso and fastened high across the chest. Its collar rose modestly around the neck rather than folding outward, and narrow leather edging reinforced the front and cuffs. One side had been sliced open along with the coat. Beneath that was what remained of a linen shirt, once cream-colored and of considerably better cloth than the loose grey thing he wore now. Blood had ruined most of it.

His trousers were dark wool as well, fitted closely enough below the knee to disappear neatly into his boots without the excess cloth worn by sailors aboard the Pottermore. A narrow leather belt completed the pile, its buckle plain iron worked into a slightly angular shape. The leather had softened around the holes from long use.

“Was I rich?” Polonius asked as he turned the buckle over.

Gustavo, who had been leaning against the doorframe, looked at the clothes again. “Dressed like this? Certainly not, friend.” He pushed himself away from the frame and came nearer, picking up the doublet between two fingers. “But you weren’t poor, either.”

“That is remarkably precise.”

“It is remarkably obvious,” Gustavo retorted while he rubbed the wool between finger and thumb. “A rich man would have made certain you knew he was rich before he opened his mouth. Silk somewhere. Silver somewhere else. A ridiculous amount of stitching.” He dropped the doublet. “You had good wool. Good linen. Good boots. Someone shaped that coat to you instead of cutting a hole in a blanket and calling the matter settled. And you've worn all of it enough that none of it was purchased yesterday.”

Polonius looked down at the bundle again. “So what does that make me?”

“Someone with enough coin to care how he looked.”

“That sounds rich.”

“It sounds like a young man in Blackwater.”

“And how do you know that?” Polonius answered curiously as he raised his eyes. He hadn’t confided the contents of the letter to Gustavo, but the man seemed to know.

Gustavo nodded toward the coat. “City clothes. Rain clothes. Something you can walk half the day in without looking like a dockhand, then step into a countinghouse without being asked to use the servants’ door. Nothing on you says lord. Nothing says guildmaster either, unless you were an unusually modest one.”

“Perhaps I was.”

“Then the arrow has improved you.”

Polonius almost smiled. He returned his attention to the coat. There were little things Gustavo had not mentioned. The cuffs were worn more heavily than the shoulders. One pocket sagged slightly lower than the other. The leather around the belt buckle had taken the curve of his body. Near the right hip, the coat's wool had been rubbed smooth where something had habitually rested against it.

A purse, perhaps. Or a blade. His hand went there without thinking. Both men noticed. Polonius froze with his fingers resting against the empty place. Gustavo's expression changed only slightly.

“What?” he asked.

“I don't know,” Polonius said, almost defensively. He wasn’t sure why.

“You reached for something.”

“I know.”

“What?”

Polonius stared at his own hand. There had been no image accompanying the movement. No sudden recollection. He hadn't remembered possessing anything at his hip. His hand had just known where to go.

“A sword,” he said eventually.

Gustavo considered that. “Could be.”

“Was there one?”

“Not when you came aboard.”

Polonius looked up at him. “No sword?”

“No sword. No scabbard. No sword-belt.” Gustavo gestured toward the things spread across the mattress. “You arrived with what you're looking at. Robbed, perhaps, or lost any other thousands of ways.”

Polonius looked back at his hand. It still rested where a hilt ought to have been. “My hand seems to disagree.”

“That makes two parts of you with opinion, then. Your stomach has been particularly vocal this morning.”

“Three, if I count my opinion of you,” said Polonius, glancing toward the bucket.

“There he is,” Gustavo nodded, a dry smile playing on his face.

That bothered Polonius more than it should have. Not Gustavo, but the sword. Or the lack of one, rather. His hand had reached for it with a certainty his mind could not reproduce. He tried the movement again deliberately, but that spoiled it. Now he was only a man pretending to reach for a weapon he did not possess.

He searched the coat instead. There were pockets inside as well, narrow and deep, designed so their contents would not easily spill while walking. Empty now. No hidden letter. No money. Nothing bearing his name.

He examined the doublet more closely. It was almost frustratingly ordinary. Not poor, not rich. Not a soldier’s or a nobleman's. He let it fall back onto the bed.

“Can they be repaired?”

Gustavo looked at the garments. “The trousers? Certainly. The doublet, if you find someone patient. That coat?” He tilted his head. “A talented tailor could make it wearable.”

“And the shirt?”

“Burn it.”

Polonius snorted despite himself. For a little while he simply sat looking at everything. There was something peculiar about seeing the shape of himself without recognizing it. The coat narrowed where his waist narrowed. The boots bore the impression of his feet. His knees had stretched the trousers. His hands had worn the cuffs. These things knew his body intimately but he knew nothing about them.

Finally he picked up the coat again and folded it as carefully as the damage allowed.

“You're keeping it?” Gustavo mused, watching him.

“It belongs to me.”

“So I'm told.”

Polonius looked at him.

Gustavo smiled faintly. “Besides,” he said, “Blackwater has tailors enough. If you're determined to become Polonius Fritz, you may as well arrive dressed like him.”

Polonius looked back down at the blood-blackened shoulder. He wasn't certain why the words bothered him, though he couldn’t dwell on it long as a particularly violent roll of the ship made the bucket scrape several inches across the floor. Polonius caught it with his foot hurriedly.

Gustavo watched this with considerably more amusement than sympathy. “Captain says you may come above today, if you can manage it. Air will do you good.”

Some air did sound good but Polonius looked again at his old clothes. The suggestion that he might wear them died almost immediately. His shirt was beyond saving, the doublet had been cut open nearly to the breast, and even if the coat could have been forced around him, its ruined shoulder would have sat directly over the bandages. His trousers had survived rather better, but getting into anything so closely fitted while he could scarcely raise one arm promised more pain than dignity.

Gustavo seemed to follow his thoughts. “Your clothes will have to wait.”

What he wore instead had been gathered from the Pottermore's crew and looked it.

The shirt was greyed linen, loose through the body and plainly made, with sleeves broad enough that Polonius could ease his wounded arm through without having to lift it far. It smelled faintly of lye soap beneath the more persistent scents of salt and old timber. Whoever had owned it before him had possessed considerably broader shoulders.

The trousers were heavy dark wool, rougher than his own and a little short at the ankle. They gathered more loosely through the thigh before narrowing beneath the knee, where they could be tucked into boots without catching on rope or wet deckboards. There was nothing elegant about them, though Polonius found he didn’t mind.

Over the shirt went a weathered sleeveless jerkin of dark brown wool faced in leather along the edges. Its front crossed slightly to one side and fastened with mismatched ties and two surviving clasps. Gustavo had cut the opening beneath the left arm wider so that it would not press against Polonius's bandaged shoulder. The alteration left that side hanging somewhat crookedly, but it was comfortable enough.

The belt was borrowed too. Thick leather, cracked along the edges and several inches too long. Gustavo watched Polonius wrap it around himself before taking it and silently producing a small knife to punch another hole through it.

His own boots were the exception. Polonius picked one up from where it had fallen when Gustavo entered. Good dark leather, calf-high, weathered rather than neglected, with the toes scratched and the soles stained from roads he could not remember walking. The leather had softened around his ankles through long use. They fit perfectly.

Polonius sat for a moment after pulling them on, staring down at his own feet. His trousers were borrowed. His shirt was borrowed. His jerkin was borrowed. Even the belt around his waist belonged to some sailor whose name he did not know.

But the boots were his. It was an absurdly small comfort.

The coat came last. It was a great hooded shipboard thing of coarse grey-brown wool, thick enough to turn wind and light rain, with leather reinforcement across the shoulders and around the openings. It belonged to someone considerably larger than Polonius and hung heavily from him, nearly swallowing the borrowed jerkin beneath it. The sleeves had to be turned back at the wrists. When he fastened it, the coat seemed determined to make him half again as wide as he actually was.

“I look like a thief,” Polonius said as he looked down at himself. It made him smirk for some reason.

“You look like a man wearing another man's clothes.”

“Is there a distinction?”

“Usually the owner,” Gustavo smiled.

Polonius glanced toward the folded charcoal coat on the mattress.

The man who had worn that one had apparently cared how he looked. He had owned good wool, good linen, fitted leather boots and, perhaps, a sword. No, certainly he had owned a sword. Polonius was sure of it, though he couldn’t say much of why. The man standing in the cabin wore a sailor's oversized coat, another man's shirt, another man's trousers and a belt with a fresh hole punched through it because he was too narrow for its previous owner.

Only the boots connected the two, but Polonius didn’t feel any less comfortable in the borrowed attire. Polonius bent and gathered the remains of his old clothes together. The ruined shirt he hesitated over before adding it to the pile despite Gustavo's advice. He folded the doublet over it, then the trousers, and finally laid the charcoal coat across the top.

He would keep them and have them repaired in Blackwater. They were nice enough and at least better fitting than the ones he donned now.

“Coming?” Called Gustavo, opening the door.

Polonius stood and the ship immediately shifted beneath him. He caught the edge of the bed. Gustavo waited, watching patiently.

“Not a word,” Polonius mumbled.

“I said nothing,” Gustavo answered with mock offense.

“You were going to.”

Gustavo merely laughed and walked out. Polonius followed him.

The passage outside the cabin was narrow enough that Polonius could touch both walls if he spread his arms, though his shoulder prevented him from testing it. The air smelled of damp timber, tar, old smoke, unwashed bodies and something sour he eventually decided was bilge water. Hammocks hung in spaces he would not have thought large enough for men. Chests had been wedged beneath benches and tied down with rope. Every available hook held something: coats, lanterns, tools, bags, coils of line.

Men moved around Gustavo without looking at him, though they looked at Polonius. And very openly, at that. He supposed he would stare too if a nameless half-dead stranger had been deposited aboard his ship and then emerged several days later dressed in the crew's leftovers.

A broad man with reddish whiskers stopped carrying a coil of rope long enough to look Polonius over.

“Dead man walks,” he whistled.

“Disappointed?” Gustavo asked brightly.

“Had two Nails on him dying,” he said honestly.

“You wagered on me?” Polonius asked.

The sailor shrugged. “Was good odds.”

“Who has the money?”

“Lerrin.”

“Tell Lerrin I want half.”

The sailor stared at him for a heartbeat before barking a laugh and continuing down the passage. Gustavo looked sideways at Polonius but said nothing.

The ladder to the upper deck presented a greater challenge than he cared to admit. He had to climb largely one-handed, his injured arm held close against his chest, while the ship shifted beneath him with malicious inconsistency. Halfway up, a sudden roll nearly sent him backward into Gustavo, who offered a bracing hand.

“Careful.”

“I know.”

“Clearly.”

Polonius emerged into daylight and forgot his reply. The sea occupied everything. There was no shore. No distant line of land. No island, tower, sail or tree. Only water beneath a broad pale sky, grey-blue and restless, running outward in every direction until the distinction between sea and cloud became uncertain. The sight should not have surprised him. He knew what an ocean was, but just knowing proved inadequate.

Wind struck his face and pushed cold fingers beneath his borrowed coat. It smelled of salt so strongly that he could taste it. Above him the Pottermore's sails swelled and snapped, their cloth straining against ropes that ran everywhere in a confusing mess.

The ship itself was larger than he had imagined from below, though not large enough to make the surrounding sea feel any less enormous. Her deck was broad amidships and cluttered with barrels, bundled sailcloth, timber, rope and cargo secured beneath waxed coverings. One mast rose high enough that looking toward its top aggravated the ache behind his eyes. A second, shorter mast stood farther aft. Men moved among them with the unconscious balance of people who had long ago stopped noticing the ground beneath them moved.

Polonius took three steps and nearly fell. A hand caught the back of his coat.

“Sea legs,” Gustavo said.

“I know what sea legs are,” Polonius insisted. His condition made him feel child-like and although he knew Gustavo meant no real scorn, his tone rarely helped.

“Apparently your legs don't.”

Polonius freed himself and took another step more carefully.

That became the pattern of the next hour. His mind knew things his body appeared to have forgotten, or perhaps had never known at all. He knew which side of the ship was starboard when someone called it. He knew what the rudder did. He recognized a capstan, knew rope could burn flesh if allowed to run too quickly through the hands, and understood why the sails changed shape when the crew hauled upon particular lines.

He could not tie the knot a sailor showed him. That irritated him disproportionately.

“Again,” Polonius said.

The sailor, an older fellow called Edder with a nose that had been broken at least twice, unwound the rope.

“You put it through here,” he said, his voice so gravelly it sounded like a growl.

“I saw,” said Polonius as he studied the sailor’s fingers.

“Then why'd you put it there?”

“I don't know.”

Edder demonstrated again and Polonius tried again, though he still inexplicably could not grasp it. The sailor looked at Gustavo, who had settled comfortably atop a barrel nearby.

“He touched in the head?”

“Very,” nodded Gustavo.

“I can hear you both,” Polonius said, shaking his head at the two.

“That's encouraging,” Gustavo said.

On the fourth attempt the knot held. Polonius stared at it. There was no revelation. No memory of childhood fingers learning rope beside some forgotten father. He had simply learned something. Oddly, that felt better.

By midday his stomach had begun negotiating peace with the sea. He ate sitting against a stack of barrels with several of the crew: dark bread hard enough to hurt his teeth, a piece of smoked fish, onion and something resembling cheese if one were charitable.

The red-whiskered sailor from below introduced himself as Merren Vask, apparently deciding that a man who demanded half the wager placed upon his death was worth speaking to. Edder sat opposite him. Beside Edder was a boy of perhaps six-and-ten named Toman who seemed to possess the miraculous ability to consume twice as much as everyone else while remaining narrow as a mast.

“Blackwater man, then?” Merren asked.

Polonius looked at him before answering.

“Supposedly,” he said with a shrug.

“Supposedly?”

“He doesn't remember,” Gustavo explained.

Merren stopped chewing. Toman stopped chewing. Edder continued eating.

“Anything?” Merren asked.

“Anything about myself.”

“But you know things.”

“I know you're damned ugly,” he confirmed through a bite of fish.

Toman choked on his bread, though not for a laugh from what Polonius could tell. That, and the boy kept eyeing Merren nervously.

Merren stared at Polonius, then nodded gravely at Gustavo. “Mind's sound enough.”

Conversation moved on with surprising ease after that. That was something Polonius began to appreciate about sailors. They were curious, but theirs was not the delicate curiosity of people who expected complete answers. Too many people aboard carried pieces of themselves they had no interest in sharing.

They told him where they had come from.

Merren was Morholter, from an island whose name meant nothing to Polonius but which apparently produced good sailors and terrible women according to Merren, and terrible sailors and perfectly sensible women according to Edder. Toman came from the Salt Marches and had been aboard ships since thirteen. Edder was born in Blackwater itself.

That caught Polonius's attention.

“What is it like?”

Edder tore bread with his teeth. “Blackwater?”

Polonius nodded.

“Wet.”

Merren laughed.

“That’s helpful," Polonius said, a little annoyed.

“Big,” Edder amended. “Bigger than any place has business being. River runs black through the middle. Bridges everywhere. Houses on some of them. Whole streets over water. Smells like shit when the tide goes out.”

Gustavo looked offended. “You have the soul of a poet.”

“Boy asked what it's like,” Eddar said with a shrug. He pointed his bread at Polonius. “You'll see fer yourself soon enough.”

Polonius looked down at his food. Blackwater. His city, apparently. Somewhere beneath Edder's description ought to have been recognition. Instead Polonius found himself constructing the place from words like a child hearing about a distant kingdom. Black river. Bridges. Houses. Crowds.

There were other things he knew, too. The girl and Polynices. He pushed the names away.

A shout came from high above. Every sailor on deck looked up. Polonius followed their gaze.

A man perched near the masthead was pointing north. For several moments Polonius saw nothing. Then something pale broke the distant water. A pair of spouts. Another appeared farther away.

The reaction aboard the Pottermore was peculiar. Nobody cheered. Nobody seemed frightened either. They simply watched.

“Whales?” Polonius asked.

Merren nodded. “Of a sort. They’re going north.”

There was something in the way Merren said it, though Polonius couldn’t place it. Polonius looked again. At this distance the animals themselves were nearly invisible, occasional dark shapes beneath the water followed by white breath against the horizon.

“Is that unusual?” He asked.

Nobody answered immediately, though eventually Gustavo did.

“Well, not by itself.”

Polonius turned toward him. He was starting to grow tired of the non-answers that seemed to be the only thing anyone had for him.

“Everything is ‘not by itself’ with you.”

“It is a useful philosophy,” Gustavo said with an earnest nod.

Merren wiped crumbs from his beard. “Too many this year.”

“Too many whales?”

“Too many going north.”

Edder glanced at him. “Leave it, ya old fool.”

“I only said—”

“Leave it.”

Merren left it. The whales disappeared eventually. One by one the pale spouts became indistinguishable from the whitecaps. Yet Polonius noticed several sailors continued looking north long after there was nothing left to see.

That evening he remained even after the cold drove most sensible men below. The sea changed character with sunset. Grey became black. The horizon disappeared. Stars emerged overhead in numbers Polonius had somehow not expected, and for a while he stood at the rail looking at them.

He knew some of their names. That realization came quietly. His eyes moved across the sky and certain arrangements possessed meaning. The Crooked Staff. The Lantern. The Three. The Crownless King.

He knew them. He searched desperately for what came with the names but could produce nothing. Polonius had grown used to that feeling by now.

“You look disappointed,” called a familiar voice.

Polonius looked back. Gustavo had approached without him noticing.

“I know the stars.”

“Most of them are difficult to avoid,” Gustavo replied, leaning against the rail beside him.

“The Crooked Staff,” Polonius said, pointing. “There. The Lantern. That little cluster there is The Three.”

“Yes,” the thin man nodded. “You’re well studied.”

“How do I know that?”

“The same way you know what a boat is.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“No.” Gustavo looked upward. “But it is, unfortunately, the only one I have for you.”

They stood together awhile. Somewhere behind them a sailor began singing softly. Another joined him. Polonius did not know the song. He was grateful for that.

“Tell me something,” he said.

Gustavo glanced over. “Anything?”

“Something everyone knows.”

“That is an extraordinarily broad request,” said Gustavo, considering. “The King is Theodan Adermont. Third of his name, I believe it is.”

Polonius knew that. The certainty arrived immediately.

“He’s dying,” he said.

Gustavo's eyebrows lifted. “So they whisper.”

“And who rules Dentora?”

“Careful. You'll insult a Dentoran asking it like that.”

“Why?” That, Polonius found, he did not know.

“Because they choose their kings and spend half their lives explaining to southerners why that makes them cleverer than us,” Gustavo said, smiling faintly.

Polonius knew Dentora. Cold. North. Something about darkness. He reached for more and caught only fragments.

“The sun disappears. For months.”

“Quite correct.”

Polonius smiled despite himself.

“Congratulations. You know more than I might have thought.”

Polonius looked toward the black sea and was silent for several moments.

“What was I like?” He asked at last.

Gustavo's expression changed. “I don't know.”

“You knew my name.”

“I was told your name,” Gustavo reminded him.

“You've never met me before, then?”

“Not until you were carried aboard bleeding all over the deck.”

Polonius nodded slowly. There was relief in that, strangely. Gustavo knew him only as he was now. There was no invisible comparison being made between Polonius and the dead man whose name he wore.

“What do you think I was like?”

“That seems a dangerous question.”

“Why?”

“Because if I tell you that you look like a murderer, you'll spend forever wondering who you killed.”

“Do I? Look like a murderer?”

Gustavo examined him seriously. “A man who has killed, maybe. You were in a battle, after all. But more than that? I couldn’t say, for truth.” Gustavo ran a hand through his dark hair. “I think you were a tax collector. You’ve the attitude for one, certainly.”

Polonius laughed. It hurt his shoulder but he laughed anyway.

The wind strengthened during the night.

By morning the Pottermore was driving southwest beneath shortened sail, her deck slick with spray. Polonius woke sore in places he had not known could ache and discovered that the ship's movement no longer immediately turned his stomach. That felt like victory, however small.

He began spending most of each day above. There was little else to do.

His shoulder prevented useful labor, but sailors were incapable of allowing an idle pair of hands to remain completely idle. Edder taught him knots. Toman showed him how to brace himself properly when the ship rolled. Merren taught him three different ways to cheat at dice and insisted all three were traditional Morholter seamanship.

Polonius discovered he was good at dice. Not lucky, but truly good. He could watch what had been thrown, remember what remained, judge when Merren was bluffing and, after losing six Nails the first afternoon, win eight back the next.

Merren accused him of recovering selectively. Polonius considered this evidence that perhaps the Highest, the gods, fate or whatever else governed ruined men possessed a sense of humor.

He also discovered he knew money. A copper Nail felt ordinary in his palm. A silver Crown required no explanation. When Toman mentioned that twenty Crowns made a gold Throne, Polonius knew the answer before the boy finished saying it.

He knew House Adermont minted them. He knew clipped silver was worth less. He knew merchants weighed old Crowns. He knew a Throne was too much money to leave sitting openly on a tavern table. He could not remember ever buying anything.

That night Polonius dreamed for the first time. There was water. He knew that much when he woke. Only water. Perhaps a voice. But by the time he sat upright, even that was gone.

The cabin was dark. The ship groaned around him. Somewhere far beyond its timber walls came a sound so low that Polonius could not decide whether he had heard it or merely felt the hull tremble beneath his hand.

He waited, though nothing followed it. After a while he lay back down. On the little shelf beside him rested the girl's letter. Vivienne's letter. Polonius looked at it in the darkness. Blackwater waited somewhere ahead and maybe she did too. He was unsure how that made him feel.

As he lay there in thought, somewhere beyond the hull, beneath miles of black water, something enormous sang once more. This time Polonius was certain he heard it.
