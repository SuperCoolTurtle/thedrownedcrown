The dead man had drunk too much water. Savaj knew it before he climbed down from his horse.

The body lay on its side beneath a shelf of red stone, one arm crooked beneath it and the other stretched toward the empty skin beside its hand. Flies had found the eyes. The sun had done the rest.

Mazek covered his nose. "How long?" The young boy asked.

"Two days."

Mazek stared at the body for a moment. "He ran out of water?"

Savaj crouched beside it.

The dead man wore a linen shirt that had once been white, loose trousers bound at the ankles and sandals with good leather soles. No sword. No knife. The purse at his belt had been cut away. Someone had taken anything worth carrying and left the rest for the heat.

Savaj touched the empty water skin. Dry.

"Robbed?" Mazek asked.

"After."

"How can you tell?"

Savaj pointed beside the corpse. "Look."

Mazek crouched reluctantly. There were scratches in the dirt. Not footprints. Fingers. The man had dug at the earth.

"Thirst," Savaj said.

"So he ran out?"

"No."

Mazek looked at the skin again, curious. Savaj stood. The sun pressed upon his shoulders like a hand.

They had left the caravan half a mile back where the old road passed between two low ridges. From here Savaj could see the first wagons in the distance, their canvas covers white beneath the sun. Twenty-seven wagons. Forty-three animals. Thirty-eight people when they left Vassar.

Thirty-nine counting Savaj. Forty with Mazek, though the boy had attached himself to the journey so casually that Savaj occasionally forgot he had become part of the number.

"He drank it all," Savaj said.

Mazek looked down at the corpse. "Why?"

"Because he was thirsty."

"I know that," the boy said, annoyed now.

"He felt thirsty and drank until he wasn't. Then he walked. When he was thirsty again, he drank again."

Mazek looked south. There was nothing but stone and dust, offset only by the occasional thorn bush twisted nearly flat by wind. The country stretched away in broken reds and yellows, ridges rising from the earth like old bones. Farther west, heat swallowed the horizon. No trees, no smoke and certainly no water.

"He thought he'd find another well?"

"Seems so. Most do."

"Is there one?"

"Sixteen miles east."

Mazek squinted toward the empty country. "He wouldn't have made that."

Savaj only nodded. The boy was quiet for a moment. Then he glanced at the empty skin.

"How much should he have had left?"

"Enough to die farther east,” Savaj said without much interest.

Mazek looked at him.

Savaj kicked dust over the dead man's face. "Come. We ought to be back."

He had taken several steps before realizing Mazek had remained behind. The boy was kneeling beside the corpse. Savaj stopped, turning to him.

"What are you doing?"

"Checking."

"Someone already did."

"They might've missed something."

"They didn't."

Mazek continued anyway. A moment later he held something up with a grin.

"Told ya they did. Got a Nail."

Savaj frowned. "Where?"

Mazek pointed toward the dead man's mouth. Savaj stared at him.

The boy wiped the copper against his shirt. "Why would he have a coin in his mouth?"

"Someone put it there."

"Why?"

"Ask him," Savaj said, with a hint of impatience.

Mazek looked at the corpse as Savaj mounted his horse. After a moment, Mazek pocketed the Nail and climbed onto his little dun mare.

"You going to tell them?" he asked.

"About him?"

“No,” Mazek shook his head. "About the water."

Savaj turned his horse toward the road. "I've been telling them about water for five days."

"And?"

Savaj looked toward the distant wagons. "And they do not listen."

Master Corven was angry about the barrels. He had been angry about them at sunrise and remained angry when Savaj returned.

"There are six," he whined.

"Yes."

"I paid for eight."

"You brought eight," Savaj nodded.

Corven pointed at the remaining barrels. "There are six."

"Now there are six."

The merchant's face had gone pink beneath his hat. He was a Crownlander, broad in the belly and narrow everywhere else, with rings on three fingers and boots too fine for Tarkott. He had removed his wool coat on the second day and spent the fourth night shivering because he had refused to believe Savaj when told how quickly the heat abandoned the badlands after sunset.

"What happened to the other two?"

"One split."

"And the other?"

"The water went bad."

Corven stared at him. "The water went bad," he repeated.

"Yes."

"And what did you do with it?"

"Poured it out,” answered Savaj. “What else?"

That got everyone's attention. The men unloading sacks from the rear wagon stopped what they were doing, while a woman beneath the shade awning lifted her head. Corven's expression went from anger to disbelief.

"You poured away an entire barrel?" Corven blurted.

"Half. Kellen drank from it before we knew."

Everyone looked toward Kellen. The teamster was sitting beneath a wagon wheel with his trousers around his ankles and had spent most of the morning there. At the sound of his name, he raised his head weakly.

"I heard my name," he said pitifully.

"Go back to dying," Corven snapped and Kellen did.

"How does a sealed barrel of water spoil in four days?" Corven demanded.

"Bad barrel. Something in it before it was filled. Doesn't matter now."

"It matters to me."

"It would matter more if anyone else were to drink it."

That took some of the heat out of him. Corven looked toward the six remaining barrels, and for the first time his anger gave way to concern.

"Is the rest safe?"

Savaj nodded. "I checked it."

"And we're meant to trust your judgment?"

Savaj regarded him for a moment. "It is my judgement you are paying for, I think."

Corven did not like that answer, but he had nothing immediate to say to it.

"We fill again tomorrow," Savaj continued.

"At the King's Well."

"Red Well." Corrected Savaj.

"My map calls it King's Well."

"Your map is wrong then," Savaj shrugged.

Corven reached toward the leather case hanging from his saddle, as though tempted to produce the offending document and settle the matter by parchment. "It was drawn by the Royal Survey."

"I believe."

"They surveyed the road."

"They asked men who live here where the water was, wrote down what they heard, and gave half the places new names."

"It bears the Crown seal."

“That I believe too,” Savaj conceded, “but the well doesn't."

A noise like a laugh came from somewhere behind Savaj. He glanced back and found Mazek suddenly fascinated by something in the dirt.

Corven lowered his voice. "I hired you because I was told you knew this road."

"I do."

"Then perhaps you might stop treating every question as an insult."

Savaj studied him. That was fair enough.

"The Red Well is older than whatever king your surveyors named it for. The water is iron-heavy. You'll see the stone around it before you see the well, red all the way down. That's why people here call it Red Well."

Corven's expression eased slightly. "And it's still usable?"

"Last time I passed."

"When was that?"

"Eight months."

"Eight months,” Corven repeated. He had habit of that.

"Wells change."

Corven looked south, and that answer troubled him more than Savaj's sarcasm had.

"How much water do we have?"

"Enough to reach it, if we're careful."

"And if we're not?"

"We get fewer people to the well."

Corven glanced toward Kellen. "Make certain everyone understands the ration."

"I already have."

"Make them understand again."

That, at least, was sensible. Savaj nodded and turned away.

"Savaj."

He stopped.

"We have a contract," Corven said testily.

"We do."

"You are responsible for bringing this caravan safely to Qasra."

Savaj nodded his agreement.

"With its cargo," Corven continued. “All of it’s cargo.”

"If I can."

Corven's expression hardened again. "The cargo is not optional."

Savaj looked along the wagons. "No,” he agreed. “But the people are."

He left Corven staring after him.

Nahla was waiting beside the third wagon. She had wrapped a strip of pale cloth around her head and another across her mouth against the dust. Beneath the first, a thin scar ran across her forehead from near one temple almost to the other, old enough that it had faded paler than the surrounding skin.

"You enjoy making him angry," she said as he came upon her.

"Only sometimes," he said truly.

"One day he'll stop paying you."

"Then I'll stop enjoying it."

She pulled the cloth from her mouth.

Nahla was perhaps twenty, though Savaj had never asked. Tarkott aged people unevenly. Sun, hunger and work could put ten years onto a face in three. She had joined the caravan at Vassar because Corven wanted a healer, although her and Savaj had often worked together. She had laughed when she heard he called her a healer. She stitched wounds, set bones, delivered children, cut rotten flesh away and knew which plants would ease a fever and which would empty a man's bowels until there was little man left. Healer was close enough.

"How’ll Kellen do?" He asked.

"If he stops drinking from whatever cup he finds, he'll recover. He'll be weak tomorrow."

"He rides the wagon then."

"He was already riding the wagon," Nahla laughed, “and before any bad drink, too.”

Savaj glanced toward him, a light smirk upon his lips. "Anyone else?"

"Not yet,” said Nahla thankfully. “Burn his cup."

"I did."

Nahla studied him for a moment. "What did you find when you took the boy?"

"A dead man,” Savaj said, looking south. The old road was barely a road at all, two pale tracks wandering between shelves of stone. Beyond the nearest ridge, nothing moved. "He'd been alone. Two days dead, perhaps."

"Cause?"

"Thirst."

"Close to the road?"

"Close enough."

Nahla followed his gaze. "Anything else?"

Savaj did not answer immediately. There had been nothing around the corpse that should have bothered him. "No tracks worth following. Someone robbed him after he died."

"You think whoever did it is still near?"

"No, long gone more like."

Nahla waited. When he offered nothing more, she looked south with him.

Savaj watched the heat shimmer over the road. "Nothing's moving."

"At midday?"

"Nothing."

"Maybe everything with sense is in shade."

"Maybe."

She knew him well enough not to push further. "Tell me if maybe becomes something else."

"I will,” Savaj promised with a nod and went to check the animals.

They moved again when the sun had begun its descent.

Only foreigners traveled Tarkott at noon if they had a choice. The land kept heat after sundown, but the sun itself was a different beast, taking strength from a man so gradually that he might not realize what had happened until his legs stopped carrying him. Savaj preferred to travel in the late afternoon, walk several hours into darkness, sleep, rise before dawn, and walk again before sheltering through the worst of the day.

Corven had resisted the schedule for the first two days, though by the fifth he no longer did.

The caravan stretched nearly a quarter mile along the road. Its wagons carried northern cloth, worked iron, knives, salt fish, lamp oil, wine and grain, along with three locked chests Corven claimed contained coin for purchases in Qasra.

Savaj did not believe him. Coin did not need six armed men riding beside one wagon. He had transported true coin for Corven before, and then the man had taken no such precaution.

He asked what it was for true once. Corven had reminded him that he was being paid to guide, not inspect, and Savaj had left the matter there. Mostly. He knew the guarded wagon rode heavier than three chests of coin should make it. Whatever Corven carried was his concern and his only so long as nobody else decided to make it Savaj's.

He rode ahead with Mazek. Ronnel, one of Corven's hired swords, followed some distance behind, then came the wagons and three more guards, with the remaining two riding at the rear.

The Crownlanders wore mail despite the heat. Savaj had advised against it and been ignored. Men trusted iron because iron could stop a blade, he knew, though they rarely considered all the things that could kill them without blades.

Mazek pointed toward the sky. "Vultures."

Savaj looked up. "Four."

"I saw three," the boy protested.

"There's another higher up."

Mazek shaded his eyes until he found it. The birds were circling east, black specks against a sky bleached almost white.

"Dead thing?"

"Probably."

"Man?"

"Could be."

"The man we found?"

"No, wrong direction."

Mazek nodded, and they rode on. The road narrowed ahead, red walls rising on either side. They were not high enough to call it a canyon, but they were high enough that Savaj disliked them. He swung down from the saddle.

Mazek did the same. "What is it?"

"Stay here."

"I can come."

"I know, but you won’t."

The boy's expression soured, but he took Savaj's reins.

Loose stone shifted beneath Savaj's boots as he climbed the slope. At the top he crouched and studied the country ahead. Low ridges ran southward, cut by dry gullies and washes. A patch of pale grass clung to a hollow where rain had gathered months ago. The road continued through it all, two faint lines scratched across a land that seemed determined to erase them.

Savaj watched for movement as he waited. A lizard crawled from beneath a stone near his hand. Farther south, something black crossed the road, then another. Birds.

He looked east. The vultures still circled, though four had now become six. When he descended, he found Mazek chewing.

"What are you eating?"

The boy stopped. "Fig."

"Where did you get a fig?"

"I kept one."

"You said you were out."

"I was saving it," Mazek said slyly.

Savaj held out his hand.

Mazek stared at it. "What?"

"Nothing. I wanted to see whether you'd give it to me."

"No," said Mazek, a tad annoyed.

"Good,” Savaj said as he took his reins. "Stay closer to the caravan."

"Why?"

"Something's wrong."

That wiped the irritation from the boy's face. "What?"

"I don't know yet."

Behind them the caravan entered the narrow stretch of road. Savaj raised a hand and the first wagon stopped.

Corven rode forward. "What is it?"

Savaj listened, though not to him. Wind moved over stone. A wagon wheel creaked. Somewhere farther back, Kellen groaned. There was nothing else.

"The insects."

Corven looked around. "I don't hear any."

"Neither do I."

"And that means?"

"Maybe nothing."

Corven waited this time rather than arguing. "How long do you need?"

"Not long."

Nahla came forward on foot.

"What happened?"

Savaj gestured for quiet. She listened, and after a moment her eyes went to the ridges. Corven watched them with no ounce of knowing.

"No flies," she said at last.

"None." Savaj agreed.

Corven exhaled slowly. "Explain, would you? What do I pay you bunch for?"

"Flies find animals. Dung. Sweat. Dead things. There's forty of us and more animals than that, a dead man not far back, and still nothing's coming near the road."

"And what does that mean? I think it a blessing of the Three to be free of the pests."

Savaj shook his head as he dismounted. “Whatever it means, your Three have nothing to do with it.” He turned to the hired sword now. “Ronnel, come with me.”

Ronnel climbed down from his horse. He was perhaps thirty-five, with a broken nose and the patient expression of someone who had learned not to waste energy on questions that could answer themselves. He checked his sword.

"Leave it sheathed unless you need it," Savaj told him.

Ronnel gave him a look. "I know how to carry a sword."

"Good," said Savaj with a wave. It had not been the purpose of his words, but he cared little for any more explanations.

They climbed the eastern ridge together and left the road behind. From there, the vultures guided them. They found the horse first, or rather what remained of it.

Ronnel stopped. "Gods."

The animal lay in a wash between stones, much of its belly gone and one hind leg dragged several feet from the rest. The earth around the carcass had been churned by feet.

Savaj crouched beside it.

"Blood mutts?" Ronnel asked.

"Yes."

"How many?"

Savaj counted the prints. "Five. Maybe six." Human tracks ran among them, too. That changed things.

He moved slowly around the carcass, studying the ground. The blood mutts had fed here, but they had not come upon the horse wild. Human prints traveled with theirs, sometimes beside them, sometimes directly through them.

"With handlers," Savaj said after a moment.

Veyr's hand moved nearer his sword. "Cannibals?"

"Who else?"

One of the human prints was unusually broad. Another dragged slightly at the right heel. Savaj stored both away.

Ronnel looked at the half-eaten horse. "I thought blood mutts were wild."

"Some packs are."

"But Cannibals keep them?"

"They raise them."

"How?"

Savaj glanced up at him. "Same way you raise dogs, I guess."

Ronnel looked again at the tracks. His expression said he wished he had not asked.

They moved farther along the wash. A saddle lay against the stones, its straps cut. Nearby they found a broken spear and blood dried black in the dirt, but no rider. Savaj picked up the spearhead. It was cheap iron and the shaft had been snapped rather than cut.

"Where's the rider?" Ronnel asked.

"There was more than one."

"How many?"

"Three at least."

Savaj showed him the footprints. Ronnel crouched briefly, examining them, then stood. They found the first man twenty paces farther on. His throat had been opened.

The corpse wore a faded red scarf and leather armor patched at the shoulder. His knife remained clenched in his hand. Blood mutt tracks passed close beside the body, yet there were no bites upon him.

Ronnel noticed that too. "Why leave him?"

"They were told to, I’d guess."

"Can they control them that well?" Ronnel asked, disbelieving.

"Some tribes can," said Savaj offhandedly.

The second corpse lay farther up the wash. They found the third just beyond it. That one had been placed sitting upright against the stone. Savaj stopped before reaching him, but Ronnel took three more steps before he saw what had been done and froze.

Someone had arranged the dead man's hands carefully in his lap. Between them rested his own boot. His head hung forward as though he had fallen asleep while holding it.

"What in the Three…" Ronnel muttered.

"Don't touch him."

"I wasn't going to."

Savaj approached carefully. There were no blood mutt bites upon this one either. The man's other boot was missing, along with his weapons and water skin.

"Why sit him up?" Ronnel asked.

"I don't know."

"Some message?"

"Could be."

"To who? Us?"

Before Savaj could answer, a sound came from farther up the wash. Not stone this time, but breathing. It was heavy and quick. Ronnel’s sword came free while Savaj drew his knife.

A blood mutt stepped onto the ridge above them.

It stood perhaps forty paces away, low-bodied and muscular, with sparse hide stretched over reddish flesh. Pale bone covered much of its face, not the clean bone of a skull stripped by death but living armor grown outward from it, smooth in some places and ridged in others. Small irregular spikes rose from the shoulders. Its nostrils widened as it looked first at Savaj and then at Ronnel. A strip of hide had been tied around its neck.

"A collar?" Ronnel whispered.

"Yes."

The blood mutt did not attack. That was perhaps worse. It simply stood watching them, waiting. Then someone clicked his tongue beyond the ridge. The animal immediately backed away and vanished.

For all the heat around him, Savaj felt cold. "Back," he said.

Ronnel did not argue.

They walked quickly, but did not run. Running told hunters you had become prey. At the ridge Savaj looked back once. Something stood in the wash behind them, far away and thin enough that the heat distorted its shape.

Human. Or close enough. Another shape crouched beside it on four legs. The man raised one arm, though he did not wave it. His hand remained hung in the air as Savaj descended.

Neither he nor Ronnel spoke until they reached the caravan.

Corven listened without interrupting as he recounted their findings. That was how Savaj knew he was frightened.

When Savaj finished, the merchant looked south. "You're certain they're Cannibals?"

"Yes."

"And the animals belong to them."

"Those did."

Corven glanced toward Ronnel.

"Collared," the swordsman nodded.

Nahla had come closer. "Which tribe?"

"Don't know," said Savaj. “Could be any of them.”

"Marks?"

"None we could see."

"Blood mutt collars?"

"Raw hide,” he shrugged.

Corven frowned. "How many tribes are there?"

"No one knows," Nahla said.

"And they all keep these things?" Corven asked.

"No,” said Savaj with a dark look. “Some hunt with them. Some eat them. Some won't go near them."

Corven seemed to shudder. "And how many did you see?"

"One man. One mutt clearly."

"Then we don't know that there are many?" Corven began hopefully, though shut up at Savaj’s expression.

Nahla asked the more useful question. "How close?"

"Close enough to watch the road."

She nodded. "Then we turn back?"

"To the fork,” agreed Savaj. “Then east and onward."

Corven's expression tightened. "That puts us days behind."

"Two. Maybe three."

"I have contracts in Qasra."

"Then arrive late."

"Late costs money,” said Corven as he looked toward the wagons. "Could we wait until morning?"

Savaj could hardly believe the man. "No," he said firmly.

"Why?"

"Because they know where we are."

That settled over them.

Savaj looked toward the ridges. "They found those men before we did. They left the bodies near the road. They left one of their mutts watching the wash. Maybe they know about us already. Maybe they don't. I'm not sleeping here to learn which."

Corven remained silent for a while. Then he said, "I'll increase your payment if we continue."

"No."

"One Crown."

"No."

"Two."

Two Crowns was not a small amount. Savaj found himself thinking briefly of all the things he might buy with it, which was precisely why he forced himself to look south again.

"Three!" Corven persisted.

Mazek, standing nearby, turned his head sharply.

"No," repeated Savaj.

Corven stared, uncomprehending. "Why?"

"Because I can't spend it dead."

The merchant's frustration finally broke through. "You signed a contract."

"I did." Savaj held out his hand. "Give it here."

Corven hesitated before pulling the folded parchment from his satchel and passing it on.

"Clause seven," Savaj said, unfolding it. Corven’s eyes moved across the writing and stopped.

Mazek edged closer. "What does it say?"

Corven read reluctantly. "'Should the guide judge road, water, weather or hostility beyond reasonable passage, route may be altered at guide's discretion for preservation of persons and beasts.'"

Mazek looked at Savaj. "You put that in?"

"Had it put in."

Corven folded the contract. "How?"

"Paid the clerk."

The merchant gave him a long look. "You can read."

"Yes."

"You let me believe you couldn't."

"You never asked," Savaj said boredly. “And besides, after the stunt you pulled the last time it felt necessary.”

Corven almost said something. Then he thought better of it, tucked the contract away and looked back toward the road.

"East, then."

Savaj nodded, though he froze as the whistle cut through air. One note, high and far away. It came again, closer the second time.

The sound was not meant for men. Something barked from the ridge. A blood mutt. Another answered behind the caravan.

Ronnel drew his sword.

Savaj was already turning. "Move the wagons!"

Corven looked toward the ridge. "They're here?"

"Turn them!" He called again without answering.

Drivers shouted and reins snapped as men tried to reverse twenty-seven wagons on a road barely wide enough for two. Savaj had known how little room there was when he chose the place to stop and now he hated himself for it.

"First six forward!" he shouted. "There's a wash ahead. Turn there. Rest back one at a time!"

The drivers stared at him.

"Go!"

They moved and only moments after a scream came from the rear. Human. Then a horse began shrieking. Savaj ran towards them.

Mazek followed.

"Stay with Corven!" Savaj yelled back at him.

"I'm coming," the young boy argued.

"Mazek!" Something in Savaj's voice stopped him. He pointed toward the merchant. "Corven. Now!"

For once the boy obeyed.

Another whistle sounded, two short notes this time, and a deep barking erupted from the western ridge. Savaj reached the last wagon.

One of the rear guards was on the ground, an arrow jutting from his shoulder. His horse was no longer standing. A blood mutt had it by the muzzle, pale skull pressed against the horse's face while the animal reared and twisted, screaming through its jaws. A second mutt struck at the hindquarters, and Savaj understood almost at once what they were doing.

The first pulled as the second drove and together they brought the horse down. The wounded guard scrambled backward on one elbow. Savaj drew his knife and shouted.

The nearest mutt looked at him. Its face was almost entirely pale skull, dark eyes set deep behind the bone. It released the horse. For one hopeful heartbeat Savaj thought it might retreat.

Another whistle sounded. The animal turned immediately toward the wounded man.

Savaj understood, too late. "No!" He cried.

The mutt lunged.

Ronnel arrived first. His sword struck across its shoulder and sent the creature rolling, but it regained its feet almost before it had stopped moving and came at him without hesitation. There was no yelp, no retreat, no bewilderment at the pain. The second mutt began circling behind him.

"Spear!" Savaj shouted.

A guard threw one. Savaj caught it badly, nearly lost it, then recovered and thrust the point between himself and the circling animal. The mutt stopped, lips drawing back from teeth that were too exposed beneath the bony mask of its face.

A whistle split the air again and both animals withdrew at once, though not far. They paced just beyond reach, stalking.

Nahla reached the wounded guard. "Get him under the wagon."

Another arrow struck the boards above her. Then another.

Savaj could see no bowmen. The ridges remained empty. South of them, figures appeared on the road. Five at first, then six.

Thin bodies, bare legs, scraps of hide and cloth hanging from their shoulders. One walked with a slight drag in his right heel. Savaj recognized the print from the wash.

The figure stopped and turned its head toward him. It had seen him there too, Savaj knew it. One of the others crouched and placed both hands against the ground. Another made a clicking noise deep in his throat.

The blood mutts answered with eager whines.

Ronnel looked from the animals to the men in shock. "They're directing them."

Corven had come after Savaj despite being told to remain forward, Mazek quickly at his heel.

"How many?"

Savaj listened. Clicks came from the western ridge. Another answered from the east. A third sounded somewhere behind them.

"Enough," he said. A persistent sweat sat upon his brow and it was no work of the sun.

The six men on the road did not advance. They only watched. One carried a short bow, another a stone-headed axe. The largest wore strips of hide across his chest, and beneath his throat something pale swung on a cord.

Savaj looked more closely. It was fingers. Human fingers, dried by the heat and threaded together.

Corven saw them too. His face changed and Savaj thought he saw tears well in the corners of his big eyes.

One of the Cannibals smiled. At least Savaj thought it smiled. It’s altered jaw made the expression difficult to read. It opened its mouth.

"Wa—" The sound caught somewhere in its throat. The Cannibal struck its chest with one fist.

"Wa!" Another answered from the ridge.

Then another. The sound traveled around the caravan.

"Wa!"

"Wa!"

"Wa!"

Corven's voice was barely a whisper. "What are they saying?"

Savaj did not know. He wished they had been growling. The attempt at speech was worse. One of Corven's guards raised his bow.

"Don't," Savaj said.

"They're standing in the open," the guard said, hesitating.

"They want you looking at them."

A child screamed among the wagons. There were two children in the caravan. Savaj had almost forgotten. Something moved fast beneath the wagons. A blood mutt. It had crawled under the axle line while everyone watched the road.

"Under!" Savaj and the guard shouted together.

But it was too late. The animal burst between the wheels, scattering people in every direction. It ignored the nearest man and passed a woman who fell directly beside it. It went for the screaming child.

Savaj ran, but the boy's mother reached him first and dragged him backward by his shirt. The mutt struck her instead, and both disappeared behind a wheel. The child screamed louder as men shouted.

A spear stabbed downward and missed. The blood mutt came out the other side with cloth clenched between its jaws. The woman still moved beneath the wagon. Nahla went for her.

Another mutt came from beneath the next cart, followed by another. They had been waiting beneath the abandoned shade of the rocks, or else had crawled in while Savaj spoke with Corven and the guard. He could not tell which possibility he hated more.

"Close the gaps!" he shouted. "Spears low! Don't chase them!"

The mutts darted between the wheels. One snapped at a mule's legs and withdrew before the animal could kick. Another seized a man's forearm when he reached beneath a wagon. The man screamed as the mutt pulled, though it was not trying to kill him, only drag him out. Hands caught the man's belt, and for several heartbeats men pulled one way while the animal pulled the other, the guard screaming between them.

Then a Cannibal dropped from the wagon roof, landing among them. Nobody had seen him climb onto it. A guard froze. The Cannibal struck him once with a stone club, splitting his face, and turned on the others.

Up close, Savaj could see the face clearly. The jaw was wrong, too heavy and thrust too far forward, with teeth crowded toward the front. Old scars ran from the corners of the mouth where the flesh had split years before and healed badly.

The eyes were human. That was the part Savaj found most disturbing. They were alert. Intent.

Ronnel drove his sword into the man's side. The Cannibal made a sound through clenched teeth but did not fall. Instead he seized Ronnel's wrist. Ronnel smashed the pommel into his face once, then again, yet the hand remained closed around him.

A blood mutt came immediately. Not at Ronnel directly, but at his sword arm. Savaj understood the tactic a heartbeat before it happened. He drove the spear across the mutt's path, forcing the creature to twist aside.

Ronnel tore himself free at last. The Cannibal staggered, and another guard cut him down. For a moment the body lay still. One of the Blood Mutts rushed toward it.

The guard raised his spear, expecting the animal to feed, but it didn't. The mutt planted itself over the fallen Cannibal and snarled at the men around him. Savaj stared.

Then, suddenly, the Cannibal moved. Only barely, but one hand crawled across the dirt all the same. The blood mutt remained above him. A whistle sounded from the ridge and as if by command the animal seized the Cannibal by the hide straps across his shoulder and began dragging him away.

Ronnel stared too. "They're taking their wounded," he said. It was not a question.

"Yes," Savaj said stupidly as he watched the animal dragged its handler beneath the wagon and disappeared. The horror of it settled differently after that, though he shook his head clear. "Keep the children inside!" Savaj shouted.

The Cannibals on the road began to move, almost as if they recognized the word. There was no charge. They spread apart instead, while the man wearing the necklace of fingers pointed toward the wagons.

Every blood mutt Savaj could see became still. Six, no, seven. There had been seven all along.

The leader made a sound deep in his throat. "Kh!"

Every mutt lunged at once. Chaos took the road. One vanished beneath a wagon. Two struck the mule team together. Another came directly at Ronnel. Men stabbed downward while animals screamed and arrows fell from the ridges.

A driver jumped from his seat and was struck before his feet touched the ground. One Cannibal came through the dust carrying no weapon at all. He seized the fallen driver by the ankles and began dragging him south.

The man was alive. Savaj knew because he began kicking. Two more Cannibals emerged to help. The driver twisted onto his stomach and clawed at the road, screaming for the men around him.

Ronnel started toward him.

Savaj caught his arm. "No," he said with a hopeless shake.

"He's alive!"

"I know," Savaj said, looking down.

"We can reach him!"

"No."

The driver dug his fingers into the dirt. One of the Cannibals struck his hand with a stone until his fingers were smashed and his grip broke.

Ronnel pulled against Savaj. "Let me go!"

"If you go, they take you too, damn it!" Savaj said, firmening his grip.

The driver screamed Ronnel's name. That nearly did it. Ronnel’s face changed, and for a moment Savaj thought he would have to put him on the ground. Instead he held on.

The Cannibals dragged the man beyond the abandoned wagons. A blood mutt trotted beside them. Then the dust hid all four. The screaming continued, longer than Savaj wanted it to. He held his breath until it stopped.

Ronnel shoved him away. "You left him!"

"Yes.” Savaj said. It was all he could say.

An arrow struck the wagon beside them.

Savaj grabbed Ronnel's shoulder. "Hate me later."

The swordsman looked as though he might kill him. Then another mutt came through the dust. A spear stuck out of its back. Ronnel turned and killed it. This one screamed. The sound was high and terrible, more dog than monster.

The effect upon the Cannibals was immediate. Three voices answered. Not words, but anguished barking cries. One of them on the road charged towards the dying mutt. He reached it beneath a rain of arrows and dropped to his knees. One hand settled against the animal's pale skull.

For one strange heartbeat, man and beast were simply there together amid the dust. Then an arrow entered the Cannibal's back. He jerked as another struck him. He folded over the mutt finally, as the other Cannibals screamed.

It seem rage alone to Savaj, but something closer to grief. He felt something colder than fear. They loved the animals. In whatever manner they were still capable of loving anything, they loved them. And now they were furious.

The leader raised both arms. The guttural cries stopped at once, even the remaining blood mutts went still.

Then a horn sounded from somewhere beyond the ridge. Low and deep. One blow, loud and carrying. The leader lowered an arm and the Cannibals withdrew.

They did not flee. There was no sudden breaking of courage, no scramble to escape. They took their wounded with them. They took two of their dead, and two of the caravans too. One of the blood mutts helped drag a body away by the hide wrapped around its shoulders.

They left the dead animal behind only because Ronnel stood over it with his sword.

Within moments the ridges began swallowing them. The leader was the last to leave the road. He looked toward Ronnel, then toward the dead mutt, and finally his eyes settled upon Savaj. He touched two fingers to his own forehead.

Savaj did not know what the gesture meant and produced scarce answer as he watched the man disappear.

Nobody cheered and nobody spoke. For a long while the only sounds were wounded animals, men breathing hard, and a woman crying beneath a wagon.

Then, from somewhere far beyond the southern ridge, the captured driver screamed again. Just once. The caravan went completely still. Ronnel closed his eyes and turned away from Savaj.

Another sound followed, though not a scream. A series of short guttural calls. Cannibal voices. Then nothing.

Savaj looked at the road. "Move." When nobody responded, he raised his voice. "Move the fucking wagons!"

They moved then.

They traveled until moonrise, and no one complained about the pace. Even Corven had lost his appetite for argument.

Six wagons were gone. Two men were dead, one had been taken, and two more were wounded badly enough that Nahla refused to let them walk. They had lost two mules and three water skins. One blood mutt lay dead on the road behind them.

They made camp without fires in a hollow surrounded by low stone. No one liked that either, but no one argued. Nahla worked beneath a covered lamp while Mazek sat nearby with his knife across his knees. The boy had barely spoken since the attack.

Savaj walked the perimeter. There were no whistles, no tracks and no blood mutts. This time Savaj was happy to be presented with nothing. It meant they had gotten away, or so he hoped.

When he returned, Corven was waiting. The merchant looked older. Dust had turned his expensive shirt the color of the road, and there was dried blood on one sleeve that Savaj did not think belonged to him.

"You were right," Corven said.

Savaj sat on a stone. "About turning back?"

"Yes,” said Corven as he lowered himself onto another stone.

For a while neither spoke. Then the merchant said, "They were men."

"Yes," Savaj said, looking at him a tad curiously.

"I had heard stories."

"Most have."

"I thought..." He stopped.

Savaj knew what he had thought. Everyone from the Crown thought the same thing. "That they'd look like beasts."

Corven nodded. "They don't."

"No," Savaj agreed. “They’re much worse.”

"The one that came onto the wagon looked at me."

Savaj waited.

"I mean looked at me,” Corven continued. “Not through me. He knew I was afraid." When Savaj said nothing he added, "The animals obeyed them."

"They raise them. Bred them to begin with, some say."

"Like dogs."

"Close enough."

Corven rubbed his hands together, staring at his palms. "One of them went back for the animal."

Savaj remembered the Cannibal kneeling beside the dying mutt.

"I don't know why that troubles me,” Corven confessed.

"Because you wanted them simple."

Corven looked at him.

"Men are easier to hate when you decide they aren't men," Savaj elaborated.

"You pity them?" The merchant asked.

"No." The answer came immediately. Savaj looked south. "They would have eaten us."

Neither man spoke for a pause.

Eventually Corven said, "Why did they leave?"

"Lost enough, I guess. Or gained enough, more like. They’ll make use of our dead and their own.”

Corven said a little prayer at that. “And the horn?"

"Signal, I think. From their leader, another group, someone watching from the ridge. Doesn't matter tonight."

"You don't think they were after anything particular?" Corven said, a little too pointedly.

Savaj paused. “Should they have been? More than what they took?”

"Maybe the wagon," Corven looked at him guiltily. "Seven," he specified.

Savaj waited. Seven was the heavily guarded one, the one he had been curious about previously.

Corven exhaled. "There are no coins in those chests."

"I know that much," Savaj admitted.

"How?"

"The wagon's too heavy."

The merchant studied him for a moment, and then whatever resistance remained seemed to leave him. "Steel."

Savaj's expression changed. "What kind?"

"Blades. Mostly swords. Some spearheads."

"How many?"

"One hundred and twenty swords. Twice that many spearheads."

Savaj stared at him. That was not a merchant's speculative cargo. "Who are you selling them to?"

Corven looked toward the sleeping caravan. "Azhar Kesh."

Savaj stopped moving.

"You know the name?" Corven asked, turning back to him.

"Everyone does." Savaj said carefully.

"He offered a good price."

"Azhar tends to do when someone else carries the risk."

"Is he as dangerous as they say?"

"Some of what they say is true."

Corven looked down. "I suppose that means yes."

Savaj leaned forward, forearms against his knees. "Who knew?"

"About the steel?"

"Yes."

"My factors in Vassar. The smiths. Some of the guards. Azhar's people."

"Too many," Savaj sighed.

"You think someone told the tribe?"

"No." That answer seemed to surprise Corven. "Cannibals don't need merchants telling them where travelers are. We put twenty-seven wagons on their road."

"Their road?"

"The road through their hunting ground."

"You knew?"

"I knew tribes hunted the broken country west of here. I didn't know they'd begun watching this stretch so closely,” Savaj said, his brow creased. “And besides, no Cannibals I know of work with anyone but their own tribes.”

That was different from finding them somewhere they should not have been. Tarkott had no borders worth trusting. A hunting ground could grow ten miles because a well failed, shrink twenty because another tribe took a ridge, then return to where it had been all in a matter of weeks.

Nothing about the attack required a mystery. Only opportunity and carelessness, both of which Savaj had provided.

"Could they come after us?" Corven asked.

"Yes."

"Will they?"

"Probably not tonight."

"Why?"

"They got meat."

Corven went very still. Savaj regretted the words as soon as they were spoken. Not because they were false, but because the driver and fallen guards had name. Granted, Savaj did not know them, but Crownlanders were unused to such blunt speech. Or at least unused to men being meat.

"The men they took," he corrected.

"Yes,” Corven said quietly.

Savaj had counted them for five days as man and just as easily regarded them as meat. That was another thing Tarkott did if a man let it. He stood.

"We don't stay on the road tomorrow," he said.

"Where do we go?" Corven asked.

"East."

"To the Red Well?"

"Past it."

Corven frowned. "Past it to where?"

"Kharad."

"I don't know that name."

"Most don't and better for us."

"What is it?"

"An old settlement."

"Occupied?"

"Wasn't last time."

"When?"

"Years ago."

Corven stared. "And you intend to take us there."

"We've got wounded. Less water. Fewer wagons. If the tribe follows, I'd rather have walls."

"Are the walls standing?"

"Some."

"And the well?"

"Was."

Corven looked almost offended by the answer. Then he remembered where he was. "Of course."

Savaj left him.

Mazek was still awake. The boy looked up when he approached, though he did not lift his knife from his knees.

"What was in seven?" He asked nosily.

Savaj placed a hand upon his head. "Steel."

"How much?"

"A lot."

"For who?"

"Azhar Kesh."

Mazek sat straighter. "You should've let them take it."

"Maybe, but I don’t think they knew about it."

That answer surprised him. He looked down at his knife. After a moment he said, "That man."

Savaj knew which one. "The driver."

"Did you know him?"

"No."

"He talked to me yesterday."

Savaj sat beside him.

Mazek picked at the leather around the knife hilt. "He said I cheated at dice."

"Did you?"

"Yes," Mazek said with a slight tilt of his head.

Neither smiled.

Mazek swallowed. "Could we have got him?"

Savaj looked toward Ronnel. The swordsman sat alone beyond the lamp. "No," he said.

"You're sure?"

Savaj thought for a moment. "No."

Mazek looked at him.

Savaj did not soften it. "I decided we couldn't."

The boy was quiet for a while. "What's the difference?"

"If I'm wrong, the driver."

Mazek absorbed that. "Ronnel hates you."

"For tonight."

"Maybe longer."

"Then longer,” Savaj agreed. "And I say let him. A dead man can’t hate.”

The boy looked south. "Do they really eat people?"

"Yes."

"All of them?"

"The tribes?"

Mazek shook his head. “I meant all people. Like children, too…”

Savaj looked at him. "Yes," he said.

Mazek looked down. Savaj never thought a lie to be more helpful than the truth.

"Sleep," said Savaj as he stood. He ruffled the boy's hair.

"I don't think I can."

"Then lie down anyway."

Mazek did. Savaj continued to the edge of camp. Nahla joined him sometime later. He heard her steps over the stone before she had a chance to speak.

"How are they?" He asked.

"The woman will live."

"And the guard?"

"Probably."

Savaj nodded. Moonlight caught the pale scar crossing Nahla's forehead. For a while they watched the dark together. The stars over Tarkott were hard and white. There were so many that foreigners often stared upward the first few nights.

Savaj rarely did anymore. The ground was usually more important.

"Which tribe?" Nahla asked.

"I don’t know," he sighed.

Nahla looked at him.

"The arrowheads could be anyone's," Savaj said. "And just because they were the arrowheads of a tribe doesn’t mean it was them. Could've traded for them. Could've stolen them."

"And the finger necklace?"

"Half the tribes wear something unpleasant."

Nahla accepted that. "They've been seen near this road before."

"Not this close to Vassar."

"Close enough, though." She touched the scar crossing her forehead absently. "So nothing mysterious?"

"Nothing needs to be." Savaj looked south. "They hunt. We walked through where they hunt."

"And now?"

"Now they know this caravan bites back."

"That enough?"

"Usually."

"Usually."

He looked at her. She almost smiled.

"What did Corven have?" She asked instead.

"Steel."

Nahla's expression hardened. "For?"

"Azhar."

She closed her eyes. "Of course."

"One hundred and twenty swords."

Her eyes opened again. "That many?"

"Twice the spearheads."

"Azhar's building something."

"Or expecting something."

They were quiet again.

Eventually Nahla asked, "Kharad?"

Savaj nodded.

"You've been there?"

"Once."

"How long ago?"

"Six years."

"And you're trusting a six-year-old well?" She asked, looking up at him.

"I'm trusting that it's closer than the next one."

"Fair enough," Nahla said as she cocked her head. "Corven will hate it."

"Corven hates most things."

"He followed you today."

Savaj looked at her. "That or die, wasn’t it?"

"That,” she agreed. “And because you knew the road."

"Yes," Savaj said, slow with suspicion.

Nahla did not answer. There was something in the silence. Savaj disliked it.

"What?" He said finally.

"Nothing."

"Say it."

She looked toward him. "When the arrows came, nobody asked Corven what to do."

"Corven's a merchant."

"They didn't ask Ronnel either."

Savaj looked away. "Ronnel was fighting."

"Ronnel was fighting with you. And before that, even."

"Nahla," he said warningly.

"I'm only saying what happened."

"Then stop."

She did and that irritated him more.

A sound came from the darkness. Savaj straightened. A blood mutt howled somewhere far to the south. One call, answered by another. They weren’t close but Savaj listened anyway. A third replied, the voices carrying strangely among the broken ridges.

"Same pack?" Nahla asked.

"Could be."

"With the tribe?"

"Probably."

A whistle carried faintly through the night, as if to answer her question. Three notes. The howling stopped.

Savaj listened for several minutes, but nothing else came. Mazek approached quietly behind them.

"Savaj."

"What?" He said, jumping slightly. “I told you to lie down.”

Mazek nodded apologetically. "I was, see, but Corven wants to know when we leave."

Savaj looked east. The Red Well lay there. Beyond it, several miles off the proper road, was Kharad. Old walls. A deep cistern. A settlement abandoned before Mazek had even been born.

Savaj had sheltered there once during a sandstorm, six years ago. There had been no people then, only empty houses and broken roofs and a well that still gave cold water after everything around it had died.

"Before dawn."

Mazek nodded, then lingered.

"What is Kharad?"

"Old town."

"Why'd everyone leave?"

"Don't know."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Probably."

Mazek looked dissatisfied. "More dangerous than here?"

Savaj considered the darkness. Somewhere out there, men were sleeping beside animals whose faces grew naked bone, and one of those men might presently be wearing Harlon's boots, or worse, Harlon himself.

"No," Savaj said. “Now get some sleep.”
