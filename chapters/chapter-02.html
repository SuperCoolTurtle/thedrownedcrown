The snow had been falling since before sunrise.

It came softly through the trees, fine enough that the wind sometimes carried it sideways across the beaten road. It gathered upon Rava's hood and the black mane of her mare, whitening both until she brushed herself clean.

Tarin had stopped bothering. Snow clung to his shoulders and dark hair. It made him look older from behind.

“You're turning white,” Rava told him.

Her brother glanced back. “It shouldn't be long before you are too.”

“We look ancient.”

“Like Father?”&#x9;

“I can still hear,” said Vardan Torren without turning. He rode ahead of them, but not so far out of earshot, apparently.

Tarin grinned.

At twenty and two, Tarin Torren had spent most of his life believing the world might be persuaded by sufficient confidence. He was five years older than Rava, though sometimes the distance seemed greater.

Vardan Torren sat his horse stiffly now. Rava could remember when he had ridden differently, though she had been a girl then, and memory had a habit of making fathers taller. The bearskin across his shoulders made him seem broader than he now was in his age. His hand that was free of the reins stroked his grizzled mane of black and white, the last remnants of color holding strong when his hair had long given.

Tarin rode nearest him.

Six Hearthsworn followed, along with Antravai Soran on a brown mule. Two of the younger Hearthsworn had spent much of the morning speaking with Tarin, asking him about a hunt Rava had not attended. They laughed easily with him.

Men usually did. Rava had sometimes wondered whether that was another skill required of a First. Tarin would one day be the First and the men laughed with him. He had long been called the heir, though she could hardly see him ascending the title. Their father seemed too timeless, nevermind Tarin’s attitude. He seemed to become someone else entirely when he had even a little authority to enforce.

She had asked the Antravai Soran once why he never rode a horse.

“They make a man stupid,” the old Antravai said.

“But you’re riding a mule,” Rava had pointed out.

Soren waved his hand airily. “Mules already know men are stupid, child.”

She had been fourteen then. His words meant nothing more to her now than they had that day. He still rode the same mule, or else every mule Soran owned was born old, brown and disappointed.

The road narrowed as it climbed. Black firs crowded close on either side, their branches bent beneath old snow and new. Where the wind had scoured the slopes bare, grey stone showed through in long broken teeth. Above the trees, the mountains disappeared into cloud.

This was Torren country.

Snow was nothing remarkable here, nor was it anywhere else in Dentora. There was snow in the Bright and snow in the Dark, snow upon the mountains when the valleys thawed and snow beneath the oldest firs when southern merchants claimed summer had begun. In the brightest months, sheltered valleys softened enough for black earth to show through. Men hurried seed into it while they could.

And in time, the snow reclaimed that too. The Dark was something else. More than just a harsh winter.

Not all of the country belonged to Clan Torren directly, though they guarded all the northernmost lands before the Orent, anyway. Verden and Orentar, sworn families of Clan Torren, held the snowy valleys, farms and lesser halls for their First. They owed Torren men when called, grain when assessed, judgment when asked and loyalty always.

As their Great Clan, Torren owed them much of the same. Tarin had once told her that being Torren meant men followed you. Rava had been fifteen when he’d said that. She’d asked him where, but he had only laughed at her. Rava wondered about that as they rode.

When at last they arrived, the village of Davarra had only thirty-one sacks of grain. It should have had forty. Rava counted thrice, though she knew she hadn’t mistaken the score.

“You counted wrong,” Tarin said in the tone he only directed at her when they had an audience.

“No,” she insisted firmly. Rava hated it when he did that, like he had something to prove to the men about his position.

The storehouse smelled of barley dust, old timber and mice. Oren Nar, the village headman, shifted uneasily beside the door. He was a thick-necked man with grey in his beard and flour on one sleeve. Behind him stood his wife, his eldest daughter and two boys who had been carrying sacks when the Torren party arrived.

Tarin nudged one of the sacks with his boot. “Perhaps nine learned to walk,” he said with an edge to his voice.

“Tarin,” Rava said lightly.

He glanced at Rava. “What?”

“You're frightening him.”

“The sack?” He asked, giving it another kick.

“The man,” she said, rolling her eyes at him.

Oren looked as though he wished very much to become invisible.

Tarin smiled. “If I meant to frighten him, he'd know,” he said as his hand slid to his blade's pommel.

“He knows.” Came the firm voice of their father, stood near the rear wall with Soran. Neither had spoken since Rava began counting.

Rava ran one finger down the marks on the wax tablet as she read. In wake of her father, Rava continued.

“Forty sacks assessed after harvest. Two sent to Torren Hall. Three opened for milling. One spoiled after the roof leaked.”

Oren nodded, his wrinkled brow creased.

“That leaves thirty-four.”

No one spoke.

“There are thirty-one.”

Oren looked toward Vardan. The First looked back at him. Tarin looked between the two, a mix of irritation and amusement on his face. His hand still rested at his blade. The headman swallowed.

“My sister's family came up earlier than expected,” Oren said in a small voice to Rava, though he looked towards her father.

“How many?” Rava asked.

“Seven.”

“And of them children?”

“Three.”

“And?”

“We opened another sack,” Oren confessed, in a somehow even smaller voice.

“That makes thirty-three.”

Oren's wife looked at the floor. She was very beautiful, though Rava could tell the year had taken its toll upon her.

“Who else?” Rava asked in a soft tone.

The headman said nothing. He seemed to sag in on himself. Tarin folded his arms and made to speak, though their father beat him to it.

“Oren.” The name came quietly. It was enough. Vardan Torren commanded much with very little, a feat that never failed to awe Rava.

Oren rubbed his hands together and kept his eyes down. “The Teln boy broke his leg.”

“The shepherd?” Rava knew the family. It was the Great Clans responsibility to know the people it protected.

“Aye.”

“And he cannot work?”

“Not before the Dark.”

“So you gave them grain.”

Oren nodded.

“How much?”

“Two sacks.”

That made thirty-one. Rava scratched the number into the wax and lowered the tablet. Tarin stared at her.

“That is all?” He asked sharply.

“What else?” Rava asked, exasperated. Her brother had habit of making light of serious things, and a serious matter of small ones.

“He lied.”

“He fed seven kin and a lame shepherd.”

“He lied to the First.”

Oren went pale. Their father finally moved. Vardan crossed the storehouse and took the tablet from her hand. For a moment he studied the marks.

“Why did you lie?” he asked. He looked down upon the headsmen even despite the wear age had on his body.

Oren's answer came slowly. “The assessment was forty.”

“I know my own assessment.”

“I thought—”

“That was your mistake.”

Oren lowered his eyes.

Vardan handed the tablet back to Rava. “Thirty-one?”

“Thirty-one,” she nodded.

Rava saw Tarin shake his head out of the corner of her eye at the interaction.

“Will they last?”

Rava looked at Oren. “How many mouths in Davarra through the Dark?”

“Eighty-three.”

“Eighty-one,” his wife corrected. “Old Nara died.”

“When?” Asked Rava, peering over the tablet once more.

“Six days ago. And Pell went to his wife's people at Dorran Ford.”

Rava scratched out eighty-three and inscribed eight-one next to it.

Thirty-one sacks. Two milk cows they could slaughter if they had to. Goats. Dried fish. Turnips. Some smoked venison hanging from the rafters. There would be grain in private stores as well. If it really came to it, Rava had thought she’d seen a horse or two on their way in.

“They'll live,” she said.

Tarin gave a short laugh. “A comforting promise.”

Rava said nothing. To give him words was to feed him, and Tarin was always hungry for a victory, however small. He stood straighter with the Hearthsworn watching from the doorway, resting his hand near the pommel of his blade with that easy, practiced grace. He wanted her to take the bait, to rise to the petty hook of his amusement and turn the tally of grain into a skirmish for the benefit of the headmen and the men-at-arms.

He needed them to see him as the heir, sharp-eyed and uncompromising, even if he had to bully an elder to do it. Instead, Rava let the silence stretch between them and let him wonder if she was judging him or simply ignoring him, which she knew he hated far more.

Vardan looked at Oren.

“No punishment,” he said, clapping the headsman on the back.

Oren’s head came up, more from surprise than the smack. Tarin's did too.

“But when Torren asks how much grain you have,” Vardan said, “you tell Torren how much grain you have.”

“Yes, First.”

“If your people are hungry, you tell us that too.”

Oren nodded. Vardan gave him another pat on the shoulder and turned toward the door.

“Father.”

Vardan stopped, turning to look at his son. There was something in his face that Rava could not quite place.

Tarin stood, one arm waving about as the other now firmly gripped the pommel. “He lied to you.”

“He did.”

“And that's all?” He repeated, this time to their father, and equally as sharp.

Vardan regarded his son for a moment. It was a long, level look, entirely stripped of the warmth he had for the headsmen. There was no anger in it, which was worse; it was the cold, clinical gaze of a man turning over a stone.

He saw the squared shoulders, the hand closed over the pommel. Rava was sure Tarin had gone too far, though it was a quiet voice that answered when their father finally spoke.

“He lied to save his children’s lives, and the lives of those for which he is responsible, Tarin.” Vardan said, his voice low with the gravel of forty winters. “You would have me break a man for seeing to his, just to show we have teeth?”

“We are Torren,” Tarin said, his chin lifting. “A lie to the First is a challenge.” He looked toward Oren, who seemed to have withered in place as they spoke. “Challenges should be punished, or else the mistaken challenge be forgotten.”

Oren's wife went still.

“It was no challenge,” Rava said. “And he will remember.”

Tarin glanced at her. “Will he?”

“If Father punishes him for feeding hungry people, what will he tell us next time?”

“The truth, if he has sense.”

“No. He'll hide the grain better.”

One of the Hearthsworn near the door coughed into his fist. The others around him shifted uncomfortably. Tarin noticed and it only made his jaw tighten more.

“Your sister understands him,” Vardan said, looking from his son to his daughter.

Tarin's face changed slightly. Rava knew that look. She had seen it before whenever their father compared them.

“Tarin understands him too,” she said quickly, though neither man looked at her.

“He understands the lie,” Vardan said. “You understood why it was told.”

Tarin's mouth hardened. “My sister understands everyone.”

“No,” said Vardan with an almost sad shake of his head. “She listens long enough to try, boy.”

There it was. Rava wished he had not said it. Tarin looked at their father for another moment, then gave a small nod.

“As you say, First.” He walked outside, his hand squeezed firmly around the pommel.

Vardan watched him go without a word.

Rava tucked the tablet into her belt, opposite her own blade.

“You didn't need to do that,” she said in a small voice.

Her father looked at her. “I said you listened.”

“You said it to him.”

“Would you rather I lied?”

Rava glanced toward the door. “No.”

“Then we agree.”

“We don't.”

Vardan’s mouth gave the faintest of twitches. “Go after your brother.”

Rava frowned. “Why?”

“Because he will listen to you.”

“He listens to you.”

“Not in the way he will listen to you. He hears you. Now go.” Her father retreated to where Soren waited. They broke into a hushed discussion almost at once.

Rava nodded, hating that her father was right. She moved through the storehouse with a slight drag to her step. As the younger child, it was her job to help her brother, the heir, and one she truly did not mind. She just wished Tarin was at all receptive to it.

When she finally came upon him, Tarin stood outside the storehouse tightening the girth of his horse, although it plainly did not need tightening. Fresh snow had already begun filling the hoofprints in the yard.

Rava leaned against the fence. “You're going to strangle him.”

“The horse?”

“With the saddle.”

Tarin pulled once more before letting go. His gelding pawed the ground. For a moment neither of them spoke as snow settled in their dark hair.

“He does that on purpose,” Tarin said finally.

Rava knew whom he meant. “He was teaching you.”

“He is always teaching me.”

“You are his heir.”

“I had forgotten.”

Rava looked at him. For someone who glowed in the role, she thought the rueful tone he regarded his status with to be unfair. Who was he to complain?

Tarin laughed without much humor. “Don't look like that.”

“Like what?” Rava asked, her tone betraying nothing.

“Like you're about to lecture me like Father.”

“I wouldn't dare.”

“You would.”

“I'd do it better than he does.”

That finally made him smile. He leaned against the horse. “Do you think Oren should have been punished?”

“No.”

“Not even for lying?”

“Perhaps a little,” she admitted. The grain had been necessary, but the lie was bad.

“How little?”

“Make him mend the storehouse roof himself.”

“That roof needs replacing,” said Tarin, raising an eyebrow.

“Then he'll remember.”

Tarin laughed and it made Rava smile. This was the Tarin she knew better. Not the heir. Not the Hearthsworn with young men laughing behind him. Her brother.

He had taught her to ride badly and swim worse. When she was nine, he had broken a boy's nose for calling her mouse-faced. When their mother died, Tarin sat outside of her room until dawn because she had told him she wanted to be alone. The door had been his compromise.

There were things Rava disliked in him. There were things she feared for him, too. But she loved her brother all the same.

“Father thinks you're clever,” Tarin said.

“He thinks you're clever too,” she said, perhaps too fast. It wasn’t that she disagreed wholly but, well, Tarin had a more direct way about him.

“No,” he said, noticing. “He thinks I'm brave.”

“You are,” and this time her pace was perfectly normal. Tarin really was brave, braver than most men.

“That wasn't praise.”

Rava had no answer to that. She disagreed, but she knew when he had meant too. It was no praise when their father said it. Rava had overheard them arguing once a winter or two ago. An over-eager fool, she heard him call Tarin.

Tarin looked toward the storehouse. “One day he'll be dead.”

“One day everyone will,” Rava nodded slowly. She disliked hearing him say it.

“When he is,” Tarin continued, “I'll have to know what to do with men like Oren.”

“You will, and worse.”

“What if you're not there?”

Rava frowned. “Where would I be?”

“Married. South. Dead. Wherever irritating sisters go.”

Rava turned her nose up, suddenly proud. “I am the Marana of Clan Torren, not some southern lady,” she said. “And if I should be dead, I hope to haunt you.”

Tarin smiled as he mounted. “That would be useful.”

Rava watched him gather the reins. For as long as she could remember, Tarin had been going somewhere ahead of her. At six, she had followed him through Torren Hall. At ten, along forest paths she had been forbidden to take. At fourteen, she had watched him swear himself Hearthsworn. Someday she expected to stand in Antambra and watch him become First of Clan Torren.

“Come on,” Tarin said. “Unless you intend to spend the Dark here.”

They left Davarra before midday.

There had been a time, Rava had been told, when southern travelers came north near the end of the Bright and asked where the people went when the sun disappeared. Some apparently imagined that whole villages emptied themselves into wagons and fled southward before the Dark. Rava had never understood where they thought everyone was meant to go.

Davarra would remain Davarra. Oren would remain in his house. His sister's family would sleep crowded beneath his roof. The Teln boy would curse his broken leg beside his family's hearth. Eighty-one people would eat from thirty-one sacks of grain and whatever remained in their own stores, and when the last sunlight vanished behind the mountains they would light their lamps.

Then they would endure. That was what Dentorans did. The Dark changed how they lived, not where like southerners oft thought.

The highest shepherd huts would be abandoned. Animals grazing distant slopes were brought nearer the villages. Firewood was stacked safely beneath cover. Wells were checked, roofs patched, shutters repaired, and marker poles driven beside roads before fresh snow could bury them beyond finding. Journeys that took half a day beneath the Bright might require twice that beneath stars and torchlight.

But the houses remained occupied. The hearths remained lit. No man abandoned his home merely because the sun had.

Rava rode beside Tarin as the road wound downward through the firs. The snowfall had thickened again, though there was little wind. Powder gathered silently upon the branches and softened the tracks behind them.

They passed three men heading north before the road divided. Rava recognized them by the white antler fixed beneath the throat of their equally white cloaks.

The eldest was perhaps forty, broad across the shoulders and carrying a pack large enough to make Rava's back ache merely looking at it. A short bow was wrapped against the snow behind one shoulder. The two younger men carried spears, axes, coils of rope and the long iron-shod poles used to test uncertain snow.

None wore mail and none needed asking where they were going. Their attire was that of the Antlermen, Rava knew.

Tarin raised a hand. The older man returned it.

“Out ranging?” Tarin called.

The man nodded, before assuming an awkward bow. Rava had always thought the Orentar a tad ill-adjusted. Too much time in the dark, she thought.

“Far?” Her brother asked.

“Far enough.” Grumbled the older man.

Soran made a quiet sound that might have been amusement.

The three continued north. Rava turned in her saddle to watch them. Soon the falling snow reduced them to dark shapes among the trees until Rava could no longer discern them from the soft stretch of white.

“Before the Dark?” she asked.

Tarin looked at her. “What difference would that make?”

Rava supposed it wouldn't. Not where they were going.

The Orentar had been sworn to Torren longer than anyone could truly say. Their holdings lay farther north than most Doran cared to settle, where the mountains loosened their grip upon the land and the known roads became fewer. They provided more men to the Antlermen than any other family, as their fathers had before them and their fathers before that.

Some called them Rangers, others Whitecloaks or Whitewatchers. Many called them fools.

The old purpose of the Antlermen was simple enough when spoken aloud. They watched the northern approaches. They kept the last marker posts standing. They found lost travelers when there were travelers to find. They carried word south when something in the far country changed, though it rarely ever did.

What precisely they guarded against was harder to say. There had been no northern army in Rava's lifetime. No raiders ever came marching out of the Orent. Most patrols returned with very little, yet they continued north.

That was the part Rava had always found strange. Guarding the Orent was what the Antlermen was for, but exploring it was what they truly did. Every generation of the brotherhood pushed the maps a little farther. A valley given a name, a mountain measured and a frozen river followed until it disappeared beneath the earth. Sometimes a marker was driven somewhere no Dentoran marker had stood before, or at least that was the usual assumption. In an entire sea of darkness, such things were hard to prove.

The Antlermen had a practical answer, though.  They carried great lamps. Oil was counted more carefully than food on a northern expedition. A man could hunt when his provisions ran low. He could melt snow when his waterskins emptied, find his way in a sea of black, nor hunt at all.

Beyond the last Torren markers, flame became direction, distance and time together. Companies carried lanterns in numbers that seemed excessive, although each could well mean life. They marked camps with lamps hung high upon poles and counted time with special lantern wicks that would melt by the hour.

And still they went farther. Always further. That was the true work of the Antlermen, whatever the old oaths said.

Then the next company went beyond it, or so they would claim. No one agreed precisely where Dentora ended and the Orent began. There was no wall and no river that divided one from the other. There was only the light. Or rather, its absence. Rava’s father had told her once that it had no true border, shifting ever so slightly in either direction with each coming winter.

At the height of the Bright, when sunlight lay across Torren's white mountains and children played beneath a sun that seemed reluctant to ever set again, the Orent remained beneath its black sky. In the Long Dark, nothing changed there. When the Returning came and the first rim of sunlight appeared again over Dentora, no dawn followed it north.

No living Antravai knew why. There were old explanations, naturally. Some said the sun had never touched the Orent to begin with. Some said something there had refused it. Others said the darkness was older than the sun and would remain after the sun was gone.

Soran dismissed all three. When Rava had once asked which story was true, he had answered, “If we knew, they would not be stories.” She had scowled at him for that answer.

Rava watched the empty northern road.

“What do you think they're looking for?” She asked.

Tarin nudged his horse forward. “Whatever the last men didn't find.”

Before Rava could answer, Soran called from behind them, “They are looking for the end.”

Tarin glanced back. “Of what? The Orent?”

The old Antravai nodded and Rava waited for him to continue, though Soran offered nothing more.

“You don't think it has one,” she said.

The Antravai adjusted himself upon his mule.

“I think men have been walking north for a very long time,” he said finally.

“And?”

“No one has yet come back from the other side.”

Tarin laughed. “Perhaps they should try walking south.”

Soran looked at him, a curious twinkle in his eye. “One day, Maran Tarin, you will say something wise entirely by accident, I should think.”

“Then I pray you're alive to hear it.”

“Oh,” Soran said with an inclination of his head, “I intend to be.”

They rode on.

Near afternoon the road climbed again. There were eight standing stones around an ancient white tree.

Rava had passed them more times than she could count. Snow lay thick across their tops and filled the cuts in their faces. The stones bore the antramar of the Great Clans: Split Crown, Three Furrows, Broken Bough, Horned Mountain, Watching Eye, Joining Waters, Black Mast, Barred Spear. Eight hearths, one land.

Tarin dismounted and Rava followed. Their father had stopped ahead at the tree. Soran was beside him, staring north. For a moment, Rava thought of the three Antlermen again. They would still be riding that way, farther north now for certain.

“What is it?” she asked the Antravai, following his gaze.

Soran did not answer. Beyond the trees, the land dropped sharply enough that a strip of distant sea could be seen between the mountains. The Mourning Sea. From here it looked almost black beneath the white country.

“Listen,” Soran said.

Tarin came beside her. “To what?”

“Quiet.”

And for once, her brother obeyed without a word. Wind moved through the white tree and snow whispered from each laden branch. A horse stamped. Then Rava heard it.

It was a low sound, so distant she might have mistaken it for wind against the mountains. It came again, longer this time. Rava felt it more than heard it. The horses lifted their heads to it.

“What is that?” Tarin asked.

Soran continued looking toward the sea and again provided no answer.

“A whale,” Vardan said.

Rava had heard whales before. Not often this far inland, but sometimes their songs carried strangely between the mountains. There was something different this time. Another call came. Then another beneath it. Then another.

Rava could no longer tell how many there were. Tarin stepped closer to the edge of the road.

“There must be dozens,” he said as the songs continued.

“Soran?” Rava looked at the old Antravai. His face troubled her more than the sound.

“We should go,” Soran said as he turned away from the sea.

Tarin laughed uncertainly. “Afraid of whales now?”

“No.”

“Then what?” Her brother pressed.

Soran mounted his mule. “The Dark comes soon enough without waiting for it.”
